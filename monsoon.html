What Is the Monsoon? — ITCZ Migration and Cross-Equatorial Flow

Definition
Monsoon comes from Arabic mawsim (season). It is a seasonal reversal of wind direction driven by differential heating between the Indian landmass and the Indian Ocean. In summer (Apr–Sep), the land heats faster than the sea, creating a thermally-induced low pressure over the Thar Desert and Peninsular India. Moisture-laden south-westerly winds rush in from the Indian Ocean to fill this low — the Indian Summer Monsoon (ISM).
Global Context
The ISM is part of the Asian Monsoon System — the world's largest monsoon system, affecting over 4 billion people. It is driven by the ITCZ (Intertropical Convergence Zone) shifting northward past the equator as the Northern Hemisphere warms. The cross-equatorial flow from the Southern Hemisphere is deflected rightward by the Coriolis effect, arriving as south-westerly winds over Arabia and India.

GDP Linkage: A 1% deviation in monsoon rainfall from the LPA (Long Period Average) corresponds to roughly a 0.3–0.4% impact on Indian agricultural GDP. In drought years, rural distress, reduced kharif output and food price inflation ripple through the entire economy.

Early vs Late Onset
IMD classifies onset as early if Kerala onset precedes May 25, and late if it comes after June 7. A delay of even 1–2 weeks can significantly reduce kharif sowing area and cause extreme heat stress.

Early onset years: Often associated with La Niña or a positive IOD in the prior season. ENSO-neutral or La Niña conditions favour an active monsoon.

Late onset years: Typically correlate with developing El Niño events. 2002, 2009, and 2023 all featured delayed onsets with El Niño forcing.
IMD Kerala Onset Criteria (revised 2017): At least 60% of 14 designated stations in Kerala and Lakshadweep must receive rainfall ≥2.5 mm on each of 2 consecutive days, with sustained westerly winds at 925 hPa and outgoing longwave radiation (OLR) below 200 W/m².
Withdrawal: Begins from Rajasthan (NW India) around Sep 1 as the subtropical high re-establishes over NW India, pushing the monsoon trough southward. Final withdrawal from Tamil Nadu / SE coasts by mid-October.

The Monsoon System — Findlater Jet, Branches, and Monsoon Trough

The Findlater Jet
The Somali Jet (Findlater Jet) is a low-level atmospheric jet stream at 850 hPa (~1.5 km altitude) that forms in boreal summer. It carries moist air from the Southern Hemisphere across the equator over Somalia, then curves northeast across the Arabian Sea toward India.

Wind speeds reach 30–40 m/s (110–145 km/h). It is the primary moisture delivery mechanism for the monsoon. SST in the Arabian Sea helps sustain it — warmer Arabian Sea = more moisture loading.
Two Branches
The ISM splits into two branches at the southern tip of India:

Arabian Sea Branch: Strikes the Western Ghats, orographically lifts, and delivers intense rainfall to the west coast (Malabar, Konkan). The rain shadow east of the Ghats is semi-arid. The branch then deflects northward into Gujarat, Rajasthan, and eventually converges with the Bay branch.

Bay of Bengal Branch: Moves northward along the east coast, enters the Gangetic plains, and also feeds the North-East (Assam, Meghalaya).
Monsoon Trough
The monsoon trough is a line of low pressure extending from the Thar Desert (NW India) to the Bay of Bengal. It oscillates north and south on a 2–3 week cycle, controlling where rainfall concentrates.

Trough near Himalayas: Rainfall increases over the plains (active phase). Trough moves south: Plains dry out → monsoon break over NW India; heavy rain over peninsular India and BoB coasts instead.
Active / Break Cycle
Active phase: Trough is in normal/northern position. Widespread rainfall across central and northern India. Vigorous low-pressure systems in BoB. Duration: 5–10 days.

Break phase: Trough shifts to Himalayan foothills. Dry conditions over most of the Indo-Gangetic plain. Intense rain in the NE and along the west coast. Can last 5–20 days. Extended breaks = drought risk.

Active vs Break Phase — Trough Position and Rainfall Distribution

Active Phase: The monsoon trough lies near its normal position across the Indo-Gangetic Plain. Cyclonic circulation in the Bay of Bengal feeds organised convection. Widespread rain over central India (MP, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha). Most of India's seasonal rainfall accumulates during active spells.
Orographic Rainfall — Western Ghats
The Western Ghats act as an orographic barrier. Moist SW winds from the Arabian Sea branch are forced upward (orographic lifting), cooling and condensing rapidly. The Malabar coast and Konkan receive 3,000–6,000mm annually — some of the highest totals on Earth. Mawsynram (Meghalaya) similarly benefits from orographic enhancement of the Bay branch against the Khasi Hills: ~11,871mm/year.
Low Pressure Systems & Depressions
The Bay of Bengal is the world's most prolific generator of monsoon low-pressure systems. Each JJAS season sees 5–8 LPS (Low Pressure Systems), of which 3–4 intensify to depressions or cyclonic storms. These systems track NW across central India, bringing some of India's heaviest monsoon rain events, especially in Odisha, Chhattisgarh, and Madhya Pradesh.

Thermal Mechanism — Land-Sea Temperature Contrast

ENSO Link El Niño · La Niña · IOD · PDO How Pacific and Indian Ocean temperatures modulate the monsoon
🌊 ENSO is the dominant interannual modulator of the Indian Summer Monsoon. The teleconnection is robust: El Niño (warm eastern Pacific) suppresses the ISM in ~60–70% of events; La Niña enhances it in ~60% of events. However, the relationship has been weakening since the 1980s — the IOD has emerged as an increasingly important modifier.

ENSO × Monsoon — The 2×2 Interaction Grid

El Niño + Neutral IOD
High drought risk. Suppressed Walker circulation reduces cross-equatorial flow. Monsoon trough remains weak. LPS frequency drops. Historical correlation: 1877, 1965, 1972, 1987, 2002, 2009. Rainfall typically 10–20% below LPA.
El Niño + Positive IOD
IOD partially offsets. Warm western Indian Ocean enhances moisture over Arabia. Classic case: 1997 — a Super El Niño year, but the ISM was near-normal due to a strong positive IOD. IOD teleconnection via enhanced Hadley cell over India.
La Niña + Neutral IOD
Above-normal monsoon. Enhanced Walker circulation strengthens cross-equatorial flow. Active monsoon with frequent LPS. 1988, 2010, 2020 (triple-dip La Niña) — all strong to excess monsoons. Flooding risk in central and eastern India.
La Niña + Negative IOD
Mixed signals. La Niña favours excess; negative IOD (cool western IO) suppresses moisture. Net effect is often near-normal with high spatial variability. East coast may benefit while west coast sees below-normal totals.

Teleconnection Pathway — How El Niño Weakens the Monsoon

Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD)
The IOD is measured by the Dipole Mode Index (DMI) — the difference in SST between the western Indian Ocean (50–70°E, 10°S–10°N) and the eastern Indian Ocean (90–110°E, 10°S–0°N).
Positive IOD (+DMI): Warm western IO, cool eastern IO. Enhances moisture flux into India. Stronger Hadley circulation over India. 1994, 2019 are classic positive IOD years that partially offset El Niño.
Negative IOD (−DMI): Cool western IO, warm eastern IO. Can suppress the monsoon even in ENSO-neutral years. 2016 was a negative IOD year with below-average monsoon despite transition to La Niña.
PDO & AMM Influence
The Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO) modulates ENSO's influence on a multi-decadal timescale. In warm PDO phases, El Niño events tend to be more frequent and intense — amplifying their monsoon suppression signal.

Research since 2000 suggests the ENSO-Monsoon relationship has weakened in recent decades. The warming Indian Ocean means more background moisture — partially buffering El Niño droughts. But extreme El Niño events still cause severe monsoon deficits.
The 1997 Paradox
1997 was the strongest El Niño of the 20th century (ONI +2.4°C). Yet India received 102% of LPA rainfall — near-normal. The reason: a simultaneous positive IOD (+1.2°C DMI) counteracted the El Niño signal. This classic case demonstrates why ENSO alone is insufficient for seasonal forecasting.

ONI × Rainfall Anomaly Timeline — Notable Years (1877–2023)

Drought Years

1877
The Great Madras Famine
One of India's worst famines — 6–10 million deaths. Triggered by an extreme El Niño (ONI est. +2.5°C). Complete failure of SW monsoon across peninsular India and concurrent failure of NE monsoon. Colonial-era governance failures amplified deaths catastrophically.
~−20% LPA · El Niño
1972
The Drought that Transformed Policy
A severe El Niño (ONI +1.8°C) triggered a -24% anomaly — the worst drought since Independence. Led directly to the Green Revolution's acceleration, expansion of irrigation, and PDS food distribution systems.
−24% LPA · El Niño +1.8°C
1987
The Century's Worst Drought
Rainfall just 73% of LPA — only one other year in the 20th century was worse. Co-occurred with a strong El Niño (ONI +1.7°C). 300+ million affected across central and northern India. Kharif output fell ~10% nationally.
−27% LPA · El Niño +1.7°C
2002
The Policy Test
Rainfall 81% of LPA. Triggered by a moderate El Niño (ONI +1.2°C). Severe drought across Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Madhya Pradesh. Government's improved early warning systems and NDMA response prevented famines, though economic losses were severe.
−19% LPA · El Niño +1.2°C

Excess / Flood Years

2019
The Delayed Excess Monsoon
Started late (Kerala onset June 8) but ended with 110% of LPA — excess. El Niño transitioned to neutral. A strong positive IOD (+1.5°C DMI) was the key driver. August 2019 was the wettest August since 1994. Severe flooding in Kerala (second consecutive year), Maharashtra, Karnataka.
+10% LPA · Positive IOD +1.5°C
2020
La Niña-Fuelled Excess
112% of LPA — second consecutive excess year. La Niña (ONI −0.9°C) strengthened the Somali Jet and Bay of Bengal branch. Assam flooding affected ~5 million. Kerala's 2020 landslides caused by intense August rainfall. Mumbai received its highest ever 24-hour rainfall (204.8mm) in July 2020.
+12% LPA · La Niña −0.9°C
1988
La Niña Super Monsoon
Following the worst-ever El Niño drought of 1987, a strong La Niña (ONI −2.0°C) produced one of India's wettest monsoons — 131% of LPA. Catastrophic flooding in Assam and West Bengal. The dramatic swing from drought to excess in back-to-back years remains the most studied ENSO-Monsoon case study.
+31% LPA · La Niña −2.0°C

Forecasting IMD · Dynamical Models · Skill How India predicts its monsoon — and why it's so hard

IMD Long-Range Forecast (LRF) System — Inputs and Outputs

The IMD Forecasting System
IMD (India Meteorological Department) issues two seasonal forecasts: the April forecast (April 1st week) and an update in June. Both forecast rainfall as a percentage of the Long Period Average (LPA = ~880mm for all-India rainfall during JJAS).
  • 1Statistical Model (2003–2020): IMD's earlier Statistical Ensemble Forecasting System (SEFS) used six predictors: ENSO, IOD, NW Indian winter temperature, Eurasian snow cover, etc.
  • 2Dynamical Model (2021–): IMD now uses Coupled Forecast System (CFS) v2 from NCEP — a fully coupled ocean-atmosphere model run at T126 resolution. More physically consistent but retains skill limitations.
  • 3Ensemble Approach: IMD uses multi-model ensembles combining CFS, ECMWF SEAS5, and UK Met Office GloSea6 for improved skill.
Forecast Skill & Limitations
All-India seasonal rainfall (AISMR) forecasts have demonstrated skill, but regional and sub-seasonal skill remains limited. Key limitations:
The Spring Predictability Barrier: ENSO forecasts for the subsequent monsoon season (Jun–Sep) made before April have limited skill — the coupled ocean-atmosphere system is in an unpredictable state in March–April. This is the monsoon forecaster's fundamental challenge.
Chaotic Atmosphere: Even with perfect boundary conditions (SST), the monsoon's internal variability — active/break cycles, MJO phase, mid-latitude interactions — limits deterministic skill beyond 2–3 weeks.
Skill Score: IMD's seasonal forecast has a hindcast correlation of ~0.6 for all-India rainfall — meaning it explains ~36% of interannual variance. For individual states or districts, skill drops sharply.

Predictor Variables — Relative Importance for All-India Monsoon

Extended-Range Prediction (ERP)
IMD issues weekly ERP for active/break conditions 2–4 weeks ahead. These are crucial for farmers deciding sowing windows. The IITM (Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology, Pune) runs the NCUM-R ensemble for 30-day outlooks. Skill at week-2 is moderate; weeks 3–4 are generally below standard probabilistic benchmarks.
Role of MJO
The Madden-Julian Oscillation is the most important sub-seasonal modulator. Its 30–60 day cycle of convective envelope propagating eastward can enhance or suppress the ISM by ±20–30% on a 2-week timescale. MJO phase 2–3 (over Indian Ocean) = active; Phase 6–7 (over western Pacific) = break. Monitoring the MJO is now central to ERP.

Q: Explain the ENSO-Monsoon teleconnection. Why was the 1997 monsoon near-normal despite a Super El Niño?

Q: Distinguish between an active phase and a break phase of the Indian Summer Monsoon.

Q: Which meteorological parameter does IMD primarily use to assess Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), and what DMI threshold defines a positive event?
