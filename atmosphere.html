⚙️ Why Belts Alternate

☀️
Differential Solar Heating
Equatorial regions receive ~2.5× more solar energy per m² than poles, driving equatorial ascent and polar descent — the root of all pressure belt structure.
🌀
Coriolis Deflection
Poleward upper-level outflow is deflected eastward, becoming nearly zonal by 30°. This breaks single-cell circulation and creates the three-cell structure with jets at each boundary.
⬆️
Adiabatic Warming in Sinking Air
Air descending at 30° warms at ~10°C/km (dry adiabatic), becoming warm and dry. Relative humidity plummets — clouds evaporate, rain is suppressed. All major subtropical deserts sit here.

⚙️ Coriolis & Geostrophic Wind

🌀
f = 2Ω sin(φ) — Zero at Equator, Max at Poles
Deflects moving air rightward in NH, leftward in SH. Turns equatorward surface flow into NE (NH) and SE (SH) trade winds. At the equator Coriolis is zero — no deflection, hence trade-wind consistency breaks down.
🔁
Geostrophic vs Surface Wind
Free-atmosphere winds blow parallel to isobars (pressure gradient force = Coriolis). Surface friction breaks this balance — surface winds blow at ~30° across isobars toward low pressure (Ekman layer deflection).
🗺️
Foehn Mechanism
Air forced over mountains precipitates moisture on the windward side (cools at ~6°C/km saturated). On the leeward side it descends and warms at ~10°C/km dry — netting a warmer, drier air mass. A Chinook can raise temperature 20°C in minutes.

Frontal Types — Structure, Weather & Symbols

Cold Front
Dense cold air undercuts and lifts warm moist air steeply (slope ~1:100). Rapid ascent triggers intense but narrow (50–100 km) precipitation — thunderstorms, squall lines. Passes quickly (hours). Post-frontal: cold, clear, sharp pressure rise. Surface winds veer sharply (S→W in NH). Heavy rain just ahead of the front line.
Warm Front
Warm air rides up gently over retreating cold air (slope ~1:200). Gradual layered precipitation 300–500 km ahead — cirrus → altostratus → nimbostratus. Steady rain 12–36 hours. Post-frontal: warm, humid, foggy. Surface winds back (E→S in NH). Classic European grey-sky prolonged wet weather.
Occluded Front
Cold front catches the warm front, lifting the warm sector aloft. Cold occlusion: advancing cold air is colder than retreating cold air. Warm occlusion: advancing cold air rides up over retreating cold air. Complex precipitation pattern; system weakens as thermal contrast is eliminated.
Stationary Front
Neither air mass displaces the other. Persistent flooding risk as slow-moving precipitation hits the same area for days. The 2010 Pakistan floods involved a stationary front + monsoonal moisture. Often evolve into active warm or cold fronts when the synoptic pattern shifts.
Dryline: A moisture boundary — not a temperature boundary — where dry continental air meets moist maritime tropical air across a sharp humidity gradient. Most pronounced on the US southern Great Plains in spring (dew point drops 15–20°C over a few km). The single most important trigger for supercell thunderstorm and tornado development in Tornado Alley.

⚙️ Norwegian Model Life Cycle

1️⃣
Incipient Wave
A small perturbation forms on the polar front. Upper-level divergence ahead of a trough removes mass from the column, lowering surface pressure. Warm and cold air begin wrapping around the nascent low.
2️⃣
Development & Deepening
Fronts sharpen. The warm sector broadens. Pressure drops; winds strengthen. Bomb cyclogenesis if deepening exceeds 24 hPa/24 hr — driven by jet stream coupling and warm ocean SSTs beneath the storm track.
3️⃣
Occlusion & Decay
The faster cold front catches the warm front, lifting warm air aloft. The thermal contrast driving the storm is removed. The system fills and weakens over 12–48 hours, leaving a cold occluded low.

6 Formation Requirements
1. SST ≥ 26.5°C to ~50 m depth. 2. Atmospheric instability throughout troposphere. 3. High mid-troposphere humidity at ~500 hPa. 4. Coriolis force — ≥5° from equator. 5. Low vertical wind shear (<10 m/s between 200–850 hPa). 6. Pre-existing disturbance — tropical wave, MCS, or MCV. African Easterly Waves seed ~60% of Atlantic hurricanes.
Rapid Intensification
Defined as ≥35 km/h wind increase in 24 hours. Increasingly frequent as ocean heat content rises. Hurricane Michael (2018): Cat 1→Cat 5 in 36 hours. Patricia (2015): Cat 1→Cat 5 in 24 hours (945→879 hPa). A forecasting nightmare — impacts populated coastlines before evacuation is possible. Major contributor to hurricane mortality.
Storm Surge — Primary Killer
An offshore dome of water pushed ashore by onshore winds + reduced pressure. ~90% of tropical cyclone deaths are surge and flooding, not wind. Katrina: 8–9 m surge in Mississippi. Cyclone Bhola (1970, Bangladesh): 10 m surge, ~500,000 deaths. Bay of Bengal geometry makes it especially deadly — funnel-shaped, shallow continental shelf.
Climate Change Effects
Warmer SSTs and higher moisture increase peak intensity (more Cat 4–5), rainfall rates (+~25% per 1°C SST rise), and storm stalling (Harvey 2017: 1,540 mm in 4 days over Texas). Genesis zones shifting poleward. Rapid intensification events increasing. Season lengthening. The storm count may not change, but the most destructive end intensifies.

Thunderstorm Types — Organizational Spectrum

Single-Cell
Air-mass / pop-up thunderstorm
Driven purely by surface heating — no organized wind shear. Three stages: cumulus (growing), mature (updraft + downdraft coexist), dissipating (downdraft dominates). Lifespan: 30–60 minutes. Produces brief heavy rain, lightning, small hail. Common on summer afternoons in Florida (~100 storm-days/yr — world's most active region).

Multicell Cluster
Self-propagating interacting cells
Multiple cells at different stages, with new cells continuously forming along the gust front of older dissipating cells. Moderate wind shear organizes them into an overall system. Hours-long duration. Produces large hail, strong winds, flooding rain. Most common thunderstorm type globally. US Corn Belt receives ~50% of growing-season rain from MCS clusters.

Supercell
Rotating thunderstorm with persistent mesocyclone
The most dangerous storm type. Strong directional wind shear creates a rotating updraft (mesocyclone) — a column of cyclonically rotating air 2–10 km wide extending 5–10 km above the surface. Produces the largest hail (>10 cm), strongest straight-line winds, and nearly all significant (EF2+) tornadoes. Persists 2–6 hours, tracks 200–400 km. Recognizable by its rotating wall cloud and flat anvil.

⚙️ Thunderstorm Physics

📐
CAPE — Convective Available Potential Energy
Measures energy a rising parcel can extract. CAPE >1,000 J/kg = marginal severe; >2,500 = significant; >5,000 = extreme. US Great Plains regularly sees CAPE >4,000 J/kg in spring when Gulf mT air clashes with dry Pacific air. CAPE = energy accelerating updrafts to 50–80 m/s in supercells.
💧
Latent Heat Release
Water vapor condensing releases ~2,500 J/g — the primary energy source for deep convection. A single large thunderstorm releases energy equivalent to ~10 Hiroshima bombs over its lifetime. This latent heat warms the column, increases buoyancy, accelerates the updraft, and drives more evaporation.
⚡
Lightning — Charge Separation
Updrafts carry small ice crystals upward; downdrafts bring large graupel downward. Collisions transfer charge — graupel becomes negative (base), crystals positive (anvil). When electric field exceeds ~300 kV/m, lightning discharges. ~100 strikes/second globally. ~2,000 thunderstorms active at any moment on Earth.

Formation Ingredients
1. Wind shear — speed (winds accelerate with height) AND directional (direction rotates with height, e.g. SE→SW→W). Creates horizontal vorticity tubes. 2. CAPE — strong updrafts tilt vortex tubes vertical, creating the mesocyclone. 3. Low LCL (<1,000 m) — low cloud base shortens path from mesocyclone to surface. 4. CIN cap — prevents premature convection, allowing instability to build before explosive storms fire along the dryline.
Tornado Alley & Global Hot Spots
US Great Plains sees ~75% of world tornadoes — a unique geography: Rocky Mountains block Pacific moisture; Gulf of Mexico delivers warm moist mT air; jet stream provides shear; flat terrain gives storms free rein. "Dixie Alley" (SE USA) is deadlier per storm — higher population density, hilly terrain, nighttime tornadoes. Other hotspots: Bangladesh (1989 tornado: ~1,300 deaths), Argentina, South Africa, NE Europe.
Waterspouts & Landspouts
Waterspouts: Weak (EF0–EF1) tornadoes over water, often non-supercellular. Common over Florida Keys (~500–600/yr). Formed by stretching of low-level convergence zones. Landspouts: Columnar vortices extending from cumulus without a mesocyclone. Typically EF0–EF1. Both indicate powerful local low-level convergence even without supercell organization.
Record Events
Widest: El Reno, Oklahoma (2013) — 4.2 km wide. Longest track: Tri-State Tornado (1925) — 352 km through MO/IL/IN; 695 deaths; EF5. Deadliest single: Bangladesh 1989 — ~1,300 deaths. Deadliest US outbreak: Super Outbreak (Apr 25–28, 2011) — 358 tornadoes across 21 states; 321 deaths; 11 EF5s including Joplin (EF5, 161 deaths in 38 minutes).

Fog Types & Boundary Layer Structure

Radiation Fog
Nocturnal surface cooling fog
Forms on clear nights with calm winds when the surface radiates heat to space, cooling below the dew point. Most common in valleys (cold air pools). Classic examples: Central Valley California (Tule fog), Po Valley Italy, Thames Valley London. Typically burns off 1–3 hours after sunrise as solar heating re-evaporates droplets from above.

Advection Fog
Horizontal transport over cold surface
Warm, moist air moves horizontally over a cold surface, chilling to its dew point. San Francisco Bay fog: Pacific air moves over the cold California upwelling (Humboldt extension), condenses, and is drawn inland through the Golden Gate. Grand Banks fog: Gulf Stream air over the cold Labrador Current. Can persist days, not just nights.

Adiabatic cooling as air rises terrain
Moist air forced upslope cools at 10°C/km (dry adiabatic) until it reaches the dew point — the Lifting Condensation Level (LCL). Fog forms exactly at that altitude. Common on windward mountain slopes and in the Smoky Mountains. Ground-level cloud for those inside the terrain — the same as low stratus from outside.

⚙️ Boundary Layer Dynamics

🌡️
Environmental vs Adiabatic Lapse Rates
Normal atmosphere cools ~6.5°C/km (ELR). Dry air rises and cools at 10°C/km (DALR); saturated air at 6°C/km (SALR). When ELR > DALR: absolutely unstable — superadiabatic surface heating, explosive convection. When ELR < DALR: stable — suppresses convection, traps fog and pollutants below.
🏙️
Urban Heat Island & Fog
Cities are 2–5°C warmer than surrounding rural areas due to waste heat, dark surfaces, lack of vegetation. Urban heat islands suppress radiation fog within the city while rural surroundings fog up. Paradoxically, UHI moist warm air rising at night can fog the cooler suburban fringe.
🌫️
Fog vs Mist vs Haze vs Smog
Fog: visibility <1 km (liquid droplets). Mist: visibility 1–2 km. Haze: dry particles (dust, smoke, pollution). Photochemical smog (LA-type: ozone + NOx + sunlight). Sulfurous smog (London-type: SO₂ + fog). Only altitude distinguishes fog from low stratus cloud.

Stratospheric Layer Structure — Altitude Cross-Section

Polar Vortex
Stratospheric Circumpolar Cyclone — 15–50 km
A large, persistent cyclonic circulation centered on the pole in the stratosphere (not the tropospheric jet often confused with it). In winter: cold (−70°C at 30 km), tight, strong. Summer: weakens and warms. The Antarctic vortex is stronger and more symmetric than the Arctic. The ozone hole forms inside the Antarctic polar vortex — cold temperatures enable PSCs where ozone-destroying reactions occur.

Sudden Stratospheric Warming
SSW — Polar Vortex Disruption Events
Tropospheric Rossby waves propagate upward and deposit momentum, decelerating and disrupting the polar vortex. The polar stratosphere warms 30–50°C in days. The weakened/split vortex signal descends into the troposphere over 2–6 weeks, causing Arctic cold outbreaks. February 2021 Texas freeze (−18°C Dallas, $195B damage) followed an SSW. ~6 major SSW events per decade in the NH.

Quasi-Biennial Oscillation
QBO — Tropical Stratospheric Wind Oscillation
The tropical stratosphere (20–30 km) experiences alternating easterly and westerly winds that descend and reverse every ~28 months. Driven by equatorial atmospheric waves (Kelvin and Rossby-gravity waves) depositing momentum. The QBO modulates polar vortex strength: westerly QBO → stronger vortex → fewer cold outbreaks. Easterly QBO → more SSW events. Also influences Atlantic hurricane activity.

Ozone Layer
O₃ — 15–35 km altitude peak
Absorbs 97–99% of UV-B radiation. The Antarctic ozone hole (spring Sep–Nov) was caused by CFCs releasing chlorine catalytically destroying ozone inside the cold polar vortex. The Montreal Protocol (1987) has led to recovery — projected to reach pre-1980 levels by ~2065. The ozone hole also intensified the SH westerlies by cooling the polar stratosphere.

⚙️ Stratosphere–Troposphere Coupling

📡
Rossby Wave Upward Propagation
Large-amplitude planetary Rossby waves generated by topography and land-sea heating contrasts propagate upward from troposphere into stratosphere in winter (when background westerly flow permits). They break in the stratosphere, decelerating the polar vortex — the SSW trigger.
⬇️
Downward Signal — 2–6 Week Lead Time
After SSW, the weakened polar vortex signal propagates downward over 2–6 weeks via wave-mean flow interaction, reaching the tropopause and weakening the tropospheric polar jet — shifting it equatorward. This gives useful 2–6 week extended-range forecast skill for winter cold outbreaks.
🧊
Polar Stratospheric Clouds (PSCs)
Form only when stratospheric temperatures drop below −78°C (Type I) or −85°C (Type II pure ice). Exist uniquely inside the tight Antarctic polar vortex. PSCs provide surfaces for heterogeneous reactions that convert CFCs to active chlorine, which destroys ozone on spring UV exposure. Also produce spectacular nacreous (mother-of-pearl) clouds.

⚙️ AR Formation & Climate Change

💧
Integrated Vapor Transport (IVT)
ARs identified by IVT ≥250 kg/m/s. Strong ARs exceed 1,000 kg/m/s. IVT = vertically integrated product of specific humidity × wind speed. ARs form on the warm, moist conveyor belt ahead of extratropical cyclones, drawing subtropical moisture poleward in a narrow jet-like filament.
🏔️
Orographic Enhancement
When an AR hits a mountain range head-on (Sierra Nevada, Cascades, Norwegian Alps, New Zealand Alps, Andes), orographic lifting forces rapid condensation. Rainfall rates of 50–100 mm/hour are possible, 24-hour totals of 200–500 mm. The combination of AR intensity and topographic angle determines whether the event is merely wet or catastrophic.
🌡️
Clausius-Clapeyron & Future ARs
A warmer atmosphere holds ~7% more water vapor per 1°C warming. ARs are becoming wetter and more intense even if frequency stays constant. Projected: AR4/AR5 (hazardous) events increase; AR1/AR2 (beneficial) decrease proportionally. Western US and Europe face intensifying AR-driven flooding even as droughts deepen between events.

Meridional Atmospheric Circulation — Northern Hemisphere Cross-Section

Hadley Cell
Tropics — 0–30° latitude
Rising moist air at the ITCZ creates tropical rainforests. Descending dry air at ~30° creates the world's major deserts (Sahara, Arabian, Atacama, Australian). The most energetic of the three cells — directly thermally driven by solar heating of the equatorial surface.
0–30°Latitude
Thermally directType
Ferrel Cell
Mid-latitudes — 30–60° latitude
An indirect (thermally indirect) cell. Rising air at ~60° meets the polar front, creating mid-latitude cyclones. Descending at ~30°. Driven by Hadley and Polar cells. Weather in the mid-latitudes is dominated by baroclinic eddies rather than the cell's own overturning.

Polar Cell
High latitudes — 60–90°
Cold dense air sinks at the poles and flows equatorward as polar easterlies. Rising at ~60° where it meets warmer air — driving the polar front and polar vortex. The weakest of the three cells. The Antarctic polar cell is stronger and more persistent than the Arctic equivalent.

⚙️ Why Three Cells?

🌡️
Differential Solar Heating
The tropics receive far more solar radiation per unit area than the poles. This creates a temperature gradient that drives atmospheric circulation to redistribute heat — the fundamental engine of all weather.
🌀
Coriolis Deflection
On a rotating Earth, poleward-moving air is deflected eastward by the Coriolis effect. By ~30° latitude the flow becomes nearly zonal, preventing a single large cell from reaching the pole. This forces the three-cell structure.
⚡
Baroclinic Instability
In the mid-latitudes (Ferrel cell zone), the large temperature gradient creates baroclinic instability: the atmosphere breaks into cyclones and anticyclones that transfer heat poleward far more efficiently than a smooth overturning cell could.

Polar Jet
Tropopause level — 50–60° latitude · 7–12 km altitude
The dominant jet of mid-latitudes. Exists where the temperature contrast between polar and tropical air is sharpest. Speeds of 100–400 km/h. Meanders in large Rossby waves — when wavy, it traps persistent weather patterns (heat waves, cold outbreaks). Strongest in winter when the equator-to-pole gradient is greatest.

⚙️ Thermal Wind & Rossby Waves

🌡️
Thermal Wind Balance
Jet streams exist because of temperature gradients. The thermal wind equation states that wind speed increases with altitude wherever there is a horizontal temperature gradient. Where the polar front is sharpest, the jet is fastest.
🌊
Rossby Waves
The jet meanders due to conservation of potential vorticity. Air displaced poleward gains relative anticyclonic vorticity; displaced equatorward gains cyclonic vorticity. This creates westward-propagating planetary Rossby waves superimposed on the eastward jet.
🔄
Blocking & Quasi-Resonance
When Rossby wave phase speed matches the mean jet speed, waves become quasi-stationary. High-amplitude stationary Rossby waves (wavenumbers 5–8) can cause persistent blocking highs — the thermodynamic fingerprint of extreme weather events.

Polar Vortex — Stratospheric Connection

Strong Polar Vortex
A tight, cold, symmetrical stratospheric polar vortex keeps the tropospheric polar jet strong and meridionally constrained. Cold Arctic air stays locked in the Arctic. Mid-latitudes experience mild winters. North America and Europe warm.
Sudden Stratospheric Warming (SSW)
Rossby waves propagate from the troposphere into the stratosphere, depositing westerly momentum and disrupting or splitting the polar vortex. Within 1–4 weeks, the disrupted vortex descends into the troposphere, causing extreme cold outbreaks. SSW events precede ~60% of US winter cold extremes.
