Overview India's Biodiversity · Protected Area Network · Flagship Species One of 17 megadiverse nations on Earth
India's Ecological Significance: India covers just 2.4% of Earth's land area but hosts over 7–8% of the world's recorded species — 91,000 animal species and 45,500 plant species. It lies at the convergence of four biogeographic realms and contains 4 of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots: the Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, and Sundaland.

Four Biodiversity Hotspots
1. Himalaya: The world's youngest and tallest mountain range — alpine meadows, glacial lakes, coniferous & temperate broadleaf forests. Home to snow leopard, red panda, Himalayan black bear, and 10,000+ plant species.

2. Western Ghats–Sri Lanka: One of the world's richest centres of endemism. Over 5,000 species of flowering plants, 139 mammal species, 508 birds. Ghats receive 2,000–7,000 mm monsoon rainfall annually.

3. Indo-Burma: Includes Northeast India (Arunachal, Assam, Meghalaya). Hotspot for freshwater fish, chelonians, and orchids. Over 430 bird species in Eaglenest alone.

4. Sundaland (Nicobar): Nicobar Islands. Home to endemic species like Nicobar treeshrew and diverse marine ecosystems including mangroves and coral reefs.
Protected Area Categories
National Parks: Highest protection under Wildlife Protection Act 1972. No human habitation or resource use. Boundaries fixed by Parliament (e.g., Corbett, Kaziranga, Gir).

Wildlife Sanctuaries: Some human activity permitted (minor forest produce). Boundaries alterable by state government. Often form the buffer zone of national parks.

Biosphere Reserves: UNESCO designation. Three concentric zones — Core (strict), Buffer, and Transition. Conservation + sustainable human use.

Tiger Reserves: Notified under Project Tiger (1973). Critical tiger habitat (Core) + buffer zones. 55 reserves spread across 18 states. Managed by NTCA.

Conservation Reserves & Community Reserves: Newer categories (2002 WPA Amendment) with local community involvement.

National Parks 106 Parks · 44,378 km² · Strictest Protection India's crown jewels of wilderness
Legal Status: Under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, a National Park is the highest protective category. No grazing, no forest produce collection, no human habitation. Boundaries can only be altered by the state legislature. India's first national park was Jim Corbett (established 1936, then called Hailey National Park).
Jim Corbett NP
Uttarakhand · 520 km²
Est. 1936 Zone: Terai Arc Biome: Sal Forest
India's oldest national park and first Tiger Reserve (1973). Divided into 5 zones: Dhikala, Bijrani, Jhirna, Dhela, Sitabani. The Ramganga river is its lifeline. Flora: Sal (Shorea robusta), Shisham, Rohini. Dense grasslands (chaurs) interspersed with river-edge forest.

Fauna: Bengal tiger (over 260), leopard, Asiatic elephant (~600), sloth bear, gharial, mugger crocodile. Over 600 bird species incl. Pallas's fish eagle, crested serpent eagle.

Kaziranga NP
Assam · 858 km²
Est. 1974 UNESCO: 1985 Biome: Alluvial floodplain
UNESCO World Heritage Site and globally the most important single habitat for the Indian one-horned rhinoceros. Hosts 2,613 rhinos — about 70% of the world's total. Located on the southern bank of the Brahmaputra. Characterised by tall elephant grass, marshlands, and dense tropical moist broadleaf forest.

Fauna: Rhino, tiger (highest density in any Indian NP ~104/100 km²), wild buffalo, elephant, swamp deer, hoolock gibbon. Over 480 bird species. Major flood events (July–August monsoon) force animal movement south along migration corridors.

Periyar NP
Kerala · 350 km²
Est. 1982 Zone: Western Ghats Biome: Tropical moist forest
Centred on the Periyar Lake reservoir in the Cardamom Hills. Famous for wild elephants gathering at the lakeshores, especially in summer. Flora: Dense evergreen and semi-evergreen forests — rich in orchids, ferns, and medicinal plants. Teak, rosewood, and eucalyptus plantations form the periphery.

Fauna: Asian elephants (>900 in sanctuary zone), tiger, leopard, sambar, gaur, Nilgiri langur, lion-tailed macaque. Part of the Western Ghats biodiversity hotspot.

Gir NP & Sanctuary
Gujarat · 1,412 km² (total)
Est. 1965 NP Core: 258 km² Biome: Dry deciduous
The last refuge of the Asiatic lion in the world. Current population: 674 lions (2020 census). The forest is dry deciduous — teak, dhak (Butea monosperma), and khair dominant. Rivers Hiran, Shetrunji, and Shingoda provide year-round water.

Fauna: Asiatic lion, leopard, hyena, Chinkara, nilgai, four-horned antelope, marsh crocodile. 300+ bird species including Bonelli's eagle and Indian eagle-owl. The maldharis (pastoral community) coexist within the sanctuary zone.

Ranthambore NP
Rajasthan · 392 km²
Est. 1980 Tiger Reserve: 1973 Biome: Dry deciduous
One of India's most celebrated tiger parks — famous for tigers seen in daylight hunting near water bodies. Dominated by dhok (Anogeissus pendula), which turns crimson in autumn. Three ancient lakes — Padam, Malik, and Raj Bagh — attract mega-fauna during summer heat.

Fauna: Bengal tiger (~70), leopard, sloth bear, sambar, nilgai, chital, marsh crocodile. The 10th century Ranthambore Fort UNESCO heritage site stands within the park boundary.

Bharatpur (Keoladeo) NP
Rajasthan · 29 km²
Est. 1982 UNESCO: 1985 Type: Wetland / Avifauna
One of the world's greatest bird sanctuaries. A mosaic of wetlands, grasslands, scrublands, and woodlands that hosts up to 370+ bird species. Previously famous for the now-extinct-from-here Siberian crane. Today hosts bars-headed goose, painted stork, sarus crane (world's tallest flying bird), spot-billed pelican.

Flora: Kadam, jamun, babul. Wetland vegetation dominated by Paspalum grass and Nymphaea waterlilies. Winter (Oct–Feb) is peak migratory season.

Nagarhole NP
Karnataka · 643 km²
Est. 1988 Zone: Nilgiri Biosphere Biome: Moist deciduous
Part of the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve — the Nagarhole–Bandipur–Mudumalai–Wayanad complex forms the largest continuous tiger reserve in Asia. The Kabini reservoir forms its southern boundary. Nagarhole has the highest density of gaur (Indian bison) in India.

Fauna: Tiger, leopard, wild dog (Dhole), gaur (~4,000+), Asian elephant, sambar, chital, barking deer. The Kabini backwaters are India's best spot for seeing large herbivore congregations during summer.

Bandhavgarh NP
Madhya Pradesh · 448 km²
Est. 1968 Tiger Density: Highest in India Biome: Sal-forest
Believed to have India's highest density of Bengal tigers. The ancient Bandhavgarh fort (2,000+ years) stands at the park's centre. Medhi (tall grass) meadows and Sal forests alternate with rocky ridges.

Fauna: Bengal tiger (~100+), leopard, sloth bear, langur, chital, sambar, nilgai. 250+ bird species. Flora: Sal, bija (Indian laurel), dhaura, shisham, bamboo thickets. Best visited Oct–June; park closed July–October.

Sundarbans NP
West Bengal · 1,330 km²
Est. 1984 UNESCO: 1987 Biome: Mangrove Delta
The world's largest mangrove forest and one of the largest tiger reserves. Located in the Ganges–Brahmaputra delta where the river meets the Bay of Bengal. Tigers here are unique — they swim between islands and have reportedly developed salt-water tolerance.

Flora: Sundari (Heritiera fomes) — the dominant tree giving the forest its name — goran, gewa, keora. 334 plant species. Fauna: Bengal tiger (~100), Irrawaddy dolphin, estuarine crocodile, olive ridley sea turtle, water monitor, spotted deer.

Valley of Flowers NP
Uttarakhand · 88 km²
Est. 1982 UNESCO: 2005 Altitude: 3,200–6,675m
A glacial valley renowned for its meadows of endemic alpine flowers. UNESCO World Heritage Site (combined with Nanda Devi NP). Open only July–October due to snow; peak bloom is August. Flora: 500+ flowering species incl. Brahma kamal (Saussurea obvallata — State flower of Uttarakhand), Himalayan blue poppy, primulas, cobra lily.

Fauna: Snow leopard, Himalayan black bear, red fox, blue sheep (bharal), musk deer, Himalayan monal (State bird of Uttarakhand).

Manas NP
Assam · 500 km²
Est. 1990 UNESCO: 1985 Biome: Sub-Himalayan Forest
A UNESCO World Heritage Site, Biosphere Reserve, Tiger Reserve, and Elephant Reserve — one of the most multiply-designated parks globally. At the foothills of the Bhutan Himalaya, the Manas river divides India and Bhutan. Flora: Tropical semi-evergreen, mixed moist deciduous, alluvial grasslands.

Fauna: Tiger, rhino (reintroduced post-2008), golden langur (endemic to this region), pygmy hog (world's smallest wild pig), hispid hare, wild water buffalo, elephant. 449 bird species.

Pench NP
Madhya Pradesh / Maharashtra · 758 km²
Est. 1975 Inspiration: Jungle Book Biome: Teak forest
The inspiration for Rudyard Kipling's Jungle Book. Divided between two states by the Pench river. Teak-dominated dry deciduous forest with dense shrub undergrowth.

Fauna: Bengal tiger, leopard, wild dog (dhole), sloth bear, wolf, gaur, nilgai, chital, sambar, wild boar. 285 bird species. The park is famous for its habituated tigers and exceptional game viewing in the dry season.

Wildlife Sanctuaries vs. National Parks: Sanctuaries allow limited human activities such as collection of minor forest produce, grazing, and even habitation with government permission — unlike National Parks. Many sanctuaries surround national parks as buffer zones, forming wildlife corridors essential for animal movement and gene flow.

Project Elephant Reserves
India has 33 Elephant Reserves covering ~76,000 km² across 14 states, managed under Project Elephant (1992). Key reserves:

Nilgiri ER (Karnataka/TN/Kerala): Largest — ~10,480 km². Houses ~8,000 elephants — the largest contiguous population in Asia.

Eastern Dooars ER (West Bengal): Part of the terai–Dooars corridor. Connects Buxa Tiger Reserve with Bhutan forests.

Mayurbhanj ER (Odisha): ~3,700 km². Connects Simlipal with Hadgarh and Kuldiha sanctuaries.

Byrnihat–Garo Hills ER (Assam/Meghalaya): Critical for elephant movement between Garo Hills and Brahmaputra plains.
Marine Protected Areas
Gulf of Mannar MNP (Tamil Nadu): India's first marine national park. 21 islands, 3,600 km². Coral reefs (covering ~6,000 hectares), seagrass beds, mangroves. Over 3,600 species of marine flora and fauna.

Mahatma Gandhi Marine NP (Andaman): 281 km². Healthy coral reefs, sea turtles, dugong. 15 islands including Jolly Buoy and Red Skin.

Gulf of Kutch MNP (Gujarat): India's first MNP. Flamingo feeding grounds, Indo-Pacific humpback dolphin, sea turtle nesting.

Lakshadweep Marine Sanctuary: India's only coral atoll system. Spinner dolphin, whale shark, manta ray, Napoleon wrasse.
Biospheres 18 Reserves · 12 UNESCO MAB · 11 UNESCO World Network Conservation + sustainable development in harmony
UNESCO Man and the Biosphere (MAB) Programme: Biosphere reserves have three zones — (1) Core Zone: strictly protected, no human interference; (2) Buffer Zone: limited research and monitoring; (3) Transition/Cooperation Zone: sustainable land use, local communities, eco-tourism. India has 12 MAB-designated reserves, of which 11 are in UNESCO's World Network of Biosphere Reserves.
Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve
Tamil Nadu / Karnataka / Kerala · 5,520 km²
▼
Designation: India's first Biosphere Reserve (1986). UNESCO MAB since 2000.

Core Areas: Mudumalai NP, Nagarhole NP, Bandipur NP, Mukurthi NP, Silent Valley NP, Wayanad WLS.

Flora: Shola forests (montane subtropical dense forests unique to Western Ghats), grasslands, tropical dry deciduous, moist deciduous. Shola trees — Michelia nilagirica, Rhododendron arboreum. 3,300+ plant species; 132 endemic.

Fauna: Largest elephant population in Asia (~5,000), tiger, leopard, gaur, Nilgiri tahr (~2,000), lion-tailed macaque, Nilgiri langur, stripe-necked mongoose, Malabar giant squirrel. 300+ birds including Nilgiri wood-pigeon, Malabar trogon, Nilgiri flycatcher.

Indigenous Communities: Toda, Kota, Badaga, Irula, Kurumba, Paniyan — over 100,000 tribal people in the transition zone.
Nanda Devi Biosphere Reserve
Uttarakhand · 5,860 km²
▼
Designation: 1988. UNESCO World Network since 2004. UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Core: Nanda Devi NP (630 km²) + Valley of Flowers NP (88 km²).

Flora: Subtropical pine forests, temperate oak-rhododendron, subalpine birch-juniper, alpine meadows, perpetual snow. Over 500 flowering species in the Valley of Flowers, including Brahma kamal, Himalayan blue poppy, and 31 species of primulas.

Fauna: Snow leopard (key), Himalayan black bear, brown bear, musk deer, bharal (blue sheep), serow. Birds: Himalayan monal, bearded vulture, snow partridge, golden eagle, bar-tailed godwit (migratory).

Significance: Valley of Flowers open only July–September. Nanda Devi inner sanctuary closed to tourists since 1982 to allow ecological recovery.
Sundarbans Biosphere Reserve
West Bengal · 9,630 km² (India side)
▼
Designation: 1989. UNESCO World Network since 2001. UNESCO World Heritage Site (1987).

Core: Sundarbans National Park (1,330 km²).

Flora: World's largest mangrove forest. Sundari (Heritiera fomes), goran (Ceriops decandra), gewa (Excoecaria agallocha), keora (Sonneratia apetala). Trees show pneumatophores (aerial roots) as adaptations to anoxic tidal soil.

Fauna: Bengal tiger (~100 in India), estuarine crocodile, Irrawaddy dolphin, gangetic dolphin, water monitor lizard, olive ridley turtle, hawksbill turtle, horseshoe crab. 260+ bird species — kingfishers, herons, fish eagles. Sea-level Rise Threat: The Sundarbans are highly vulnerable to climate change — tidal flooding, salinity ingress, and cyclone intensification (Amphan 2020, Yaas 2021) threaten both the ecosystem and 4.5 million human inhabitants.
Gulf of Mannar Biosphere Reserve
Tamil Nadu · 10,500 km²
▼
Designation: 1989. UNESCO World Network since 2001.

Core: Gulf of Mannar Marine NP (560 km²).

Flora: 21 coral reef systems across 21 islands; seagrass meadows (Dugong dugon habitat); mangroves. 117 coral species; 150+ seaweed species.

Fauna: Dugong (India's most important dugong habitat — ~150 individuals), five sea turtle species (Olive ridley, Green, Leatherback, Hawksbill, Loggerhead), sea horse, puffer fish, whale shark, bottle-nose dolphin, humpback whale (seasonal). Climate Threat: Bleaching events (2016, 2020) severely affected coral cover. Temperature rise of 1°C above summer maximum causes mass bleaching.
Great Nicobar Biosphere Reserve
Andaman & Nicobar Islands · 885 km²
▼
Designation: 1989. UNESCO World Network since 2013.

Flora: Tropical evergreen rainforest — among the most biodiverse in India. Many species found nowhere else on Earth. Giant leatherwood (Tetrameles nudiflora), Nicobar breadfruit. Shores have coral reefs, seagrass, and mangroves.

Fauna: Leatherback turtle (largest nesting colony in India at Galathea Bay), Nicobar megapode, Nicobar treeshrew, coconut crab (largest land invertebrate), reticulated python, saltwater crocodile. Status: Currently under planning pressure — a major development project (trans-shipment port + township) has been approved for the southern area, raising serious conservation concerns.
Manas Biosphere Reserve
Assam · 2,837 km²
▼
Designation: 1989. UNESCO World Network since 2001. WHS 1985. UNESCO Heritage in Danger List (1992–2011) due to ethnic insurgency and poaching — one of the rare examples of a site removed from the Danger List after recovery.

Flora: Alluvial tall grasslands (terai), tropical moist deciduous, semi-evergreen forests. Khagra cane brakes, Bombax ceiba (silk cotton tree).

Fauna: Tiger, Indian one-horned rhino (reintroduced), pygmy hog (world's smallest, ~30 cm, endemic to this region), hispid hare, golden langur (restricted to Assam–Bhutan border area), wild water buffalo, elephant, clouded leopard. 449 bird species.

Trans-boundary: Contiguous with Royal Manas NP in Bhutan — allowing seasonal migration of elephant herds between the two countries.
Dibru-Saikhowa Biosphere Reserve
Assam · 765 km²
▼
Designation: 1986.

Unique Feature: Home to feral horses — descendants of British army horses, now wild on the river islands. One of India's most unusual wildlife sightings.

Flora: Semi-evergreen, grasslands, nalas (streams), and flood-prone river islands. Saikhowa Charali grassland is prime habitat for Gangetic dolphins in the Brahmaputra.

Fauna: Wild water buffalo (most genetically pure population), tiger, elephant, hoolock gibbon, capped langur, Gangetic river dolphin, Irrawaddy dolphin. Over 350 bird species including Bengal florican (globally vulnerable).
Cold Desert Biosphere Reserve
Himachal Pradesh (Spiti, Pin Valley) · 7,770 km²
▼
Designation: 1992.

Core: Pin Valley National Park (675 km²).

Ecosystem: Trans-Himalayan cold desert — rain shadow of the Greater Himalayas. Annual rainfall less than 200 mm. Resembles Tibetan Plateau.

Flora: Sparse — alpine cushion plants, Caragana shrubs, Ephedra, Juniperus. No tree cover. 1,008 plant species identified.

Fauna: Snow leopard (Pin Valley has one of the highest densities in India), Tibetan wolf, Himalayan ibex, bharal, yak, Tibetan gazelle, red fox, Pallas's cat, Himalayan snowcock. Snow leopard predation events (killing livestock) are common human-wildlife conflict here.

Climate: Snowbound October–April. Temperatures reach −30°C. Accessible only July–September fully.

Seasonal Guide Flora · Fauna · Climate by Season and Habitat When to see what — across India's wild landscapes
Summer
March – May
Best for wildlife: Peak game viewing — animals concentrate near water. Vegetation is sparse; sightings easiest.

Tiger parks: Corbett, Bandhavgarh, Pench, Ranthambore, Kanha — excellent. Tigers visible at water holes all day.

Elephant corridors: Kabini (Karnataka) — spectacular elephant gatherings at reservoir banks as water bodies dry up.

Birds: Resident species active; migrants have departed. Breeding for raptors — vultures, eagles nest March–May.

Rhino belt: Kaziranga and Manas — before monsoon floods. March–May accessible.

Himalayan parks: Still snowbound below 3,000m. Great Himalayan NP, Valley of Flowers inaccessible.
Monsoon
June – September
Most parks closed Jun–Sep (landslides, leeches, flooding). Exceptions:

Open: Gir NP (Gujarat — rain shadow), Rajasthan parks, Kaziranga (partly until July). Rann of Kutch wetland fills.

Kaziranga floods: Brahmaputra floods annually displace thousands of animals — rhinos, tigers, hog deer move south to Karbi Anglong hills. Critical wildlife corridors must remain open.

Western Ghats: Park entry restricted, but forests transform — lush canopy, river frogs breed, pit vipers active, king cobra nesting.

Coastal & Marine: Olive ridley turtle mass nesting (Arribada) at Gahirmatha (Odisha) and Rushikulya — May to August.

Valley of Flowers: Opens July 1 — peak bloom in August. Only 88 km² but 500+ flowering alpine species.
Post-Monsoon
October – November
Parks reopen. Vegetation lush — photography excellent with green backgrounds.

Migratory birds arrive: Bharatpur/Keoladeo opens to tens of thousands of waterbirds from Central Asia. Bar-headed geese, pochards, teals from September.

Chilika lagoon fills — Irrawaddy dolphins visible, flamingos arrive.

Siberian cranes were historically recorded at Bharatpur in October (now functionally extinct from India).

Ranthambore, Pench, Bandhavgarh — October marks park reopening after monsoon closure. Tiger sightings resume. Grass is still high, so walking trails useful.

Himalayan region: Post-monsoon is excellent — October/November. Snow leopard activity increases as snow comes down. Hemis NP (Ladakh) — leopard sightings near villages.
Winter
December – February
Best for migratory birds across India. Peak months for wetland birding.

Bharatpur: 200,000+ birds at peak. Sultanpur (Haryana), Chilika (Odisha), Vedanthangal (TN) — flamingos, geese, ducks from Russia, Mongolia, Central Asia.

Hemis NP (Ladakh): January–February — best snow leopard tracking. Animals move below snowline following their prey (bharal). World's most accessible snow leopard park in winter.

Tiger reserves: Excellent visibility in dry winter — Corbett, Bandhavgarh, Kanha, Pench. Cold drives tigers to sun in open areas; late morning sightings common.

Gir NP: November–February — Asiatic lions most visible. Cool weather = lions active longer each day.

Andaman Islands: November–April peak season — Mahatma Gandhi Marine NP. Coral reefs, sea turtles, whale sharks.

Migration Paths Birds · Mammals · Marine · Flyways Ancient journeys across sky, land, and ocean
India's Flyways: India lies under two major bird migratory flyways — the Central Asian Flyway (CAF) and the East Asian–Australasian Flyway (EAAF). Over 500 of India's 1,300 bird species are migratory — arriving from breeding grounds in Siberia, Central Asia, Mongolia, Europe, and the Arctic.

Avian Migration — Central Asian Flyway

🦢
Bar-Headed Goose
Breeds in Central Asia (Mongolia, China, Tibet) at 4,000–5,000m altitude. Winters in Indian wetlands — Bharatpur, Chilika, Loktak Lake (Manipur), Gujarat wetlands. Flies over the Himalaya — one of the world's highest migratory routes (6,000–7,000m altitude). Uses nocturnal thermals and takes advantage of jet streams in Himalayan passes.
Oct → Feb / Mar → Apr
🦩
Greater & Lesser Flamingo
Breeds in the Rann of Kutch (Gujarat) — India's only breeding colony (~1.5 million birds at Flamingo City near Bhachau). Mammal & Marine Migration

🐘
Asian Elephant — Seasonal Movement
Kaziranga→Karbi Anglong: Annual monsoon displacement — as Brahmaputra floods, ~1,200 elephants move south across NH 37 (Kaziranga–Karbi corridor). Highway crossings are extremely dangerous — 18 underpasses constructed. Movement continues until floods recede (September).
Nilgiri Elephant Corridor: Seasonal movement between Nagarhole, Bandipur, Mudumalai, and Wayanad following fruiting seasons. Elephant herds travel 50–80 km during summer.
Singhbhum Elephant Reserve (Jharkhand–West Bengal): Elephants follow seasonal fodder availability between forests and agricultural areas, causing conflict.
Year-round; peak movement Jun–Sep (floods)
🦁
Asiatic Lion — Dispersal
Gir lions have begun dispersing beyond the forest — young males establishing ranges in Girnar, Savarkundla, Mitiyala, and Ratanmahal WLS areas (all within 100 km of Gir). Population pressure (674 lions in Gir) driving dispersal. A second population was proposed at Kuno NP (MP) — one of the longest-running wildlife disputes in India, finally resolved with cheetah translocation at Kuno instead.
Dispersal ongoing
🐋
Humpback Whale & Blue Whale
Humpback whales (Arabian Sea humpback — genetically isolated population, only ~250 individuals) resident year-round in Oman Gulf / West Indian Ocean. Seen seasonally off Lakshadweep, Goa, Kerala coast. Blue whales feed in Sri Lanka–India channel (Trincomalee Canyon) during April–May. Both species highly threatened by ship strikes and noise.
Dec–Apr (peak, Indian coast)
🐢
Olive Ridley Turtle — Arribada Mass Nesting
Largest known single-species marine aggregation on Earth. Gahirmatha (Odisha): Up to 800,000 turtles nest in a single night (Arribada). Rushikulya beach: Secondary colony. Also nests at Versova (Mumbai, urban) and Agatti Island. Turtles migrate from feeding grounds across Bay of Bengal and Indian Ocean — tracked individuals travel 2,000+ km to return to natal beach.
Nesting: Jan–May (peak Feb–Mar)
🐆
Snow Leopard — Altitudinal Migration
Moves to lower elevations (2,500–3,500m) in winter following prey (bharal/blue sheep). Returns to high-altitude zones (4,000–5,500m) in summer. Hemis NP (Ladakh) tracks reveal 4–6 km daily movement ranges in winter. Individuals may travel 100+ km between seasonal ranges. Conflicts with herders increase in winter when leopards attack livestock near villages.
Descends Oct–Mar; Ascends Apr–Jun

The country has pledged to achieve 30% land under conservation by 2030 (30×30 global target), but currently stands at ~5.26%. Linear infrastructure (roads, railways, power lines, pipelines) bisects wildlife corridors, threatening connectivity essential for long-term species survival.

Climate Change Impacts
Glacial retreat: Himalayan glaciers retreating at 15–25m/year. Threatens long-term river flow — Ganga, Brahmaputra, Indus — and wetland ecosystems downstream. Snow leopard habitat is shifting upward (nowhere left to go at 6,000m).

Phenological mismatch: Flowers blooming earlier, while insect pollinators emerge later. Tiger prey breeding cycles shifting with temperature. Migratory birds arriving before/after food peaks.

Coral bleaching: Gulf of Mannar corals bleach when SST exceeds 31°C — events in 2016, 2020, 2023. Lakshadweep reefs severely affected. Acidification reduces coral calcification rates.

Sea level rise: Sundarbans losing 20–25 islands per decade. Salinity ingress kills freshwater mangroves. The iconic Sundari tree (Heritiera fomes) dying due to salinity — changing forest composition.

Monsoon intensification: Extreme floods in Kaziranga more frequent. 2022 floods submerged 90% of the park — 212 animals died including 18 rhinos.
Human-Wildlife Conflict (HWC)
Scale: India loses ~500 people per year to human-wildlife conflict (primarily elephant, tiger, leopard, crocodile, snake). Compensation paid by state governments for crop damage, livestock loss, and human casualties.

Elephant conflict: Most severe in Assam, West Bengal, Jharkhand, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh. Elephants raid crops in narrow agricultural corridors between forests. Over 500 human deaths/year attributed to elephants nationally.

Tiger conflict: Corbett, Dudhwa, Sundarbans, and Central India reserves see regular tiger attacks. Sundarbans honey collectors especially vulnerable (100+ deaths/decade).

Leopard conflict: Expanding into peri-urban areas near Mumbai (Sanjay Gandhi NP), Nashik, Uttarakhand towns. Leopards proven to suppress dog and pig populations in cities — both pest species. Complex urban coexistence debate.

Solutions: Bee-hive fences (elephants), GPS collaring, early warning SMS systems (HaathiApp), Coexistence Grants, community bio-guards.
Invasive Species Crisis
Lantana camara: Most invasive plant in Indian forests. Covers millions of hectares in tiger reserves. Forms impenetrable thickets that outcompete native grass and shrubs, reducing ungulate habitat and prey density for tigers.

Parthenium hysterophorus (Congress grass): Grassland invader. Destroys habitat of Great Indian Bustard, Florican, and grassland birds in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra.

Eichhornia crassipes (Water hyacinth): Covers Loktak Lake (Manipur), Chilika, Kerala backwaters — reducing open water habitat for waterbirds and fish.

African catfish (Clarias gariepinus): Invasive in Ganga, Yamuna — severely impacts native fish populations on which Gangetic dolphin and gharial depend.

Burmese python: Escaped exotic pets breeding in Andaman forests — threat to endemic fauna.
Poaching & Illegal Wildlife Trade
Tiger: Despite Project Tiger's success, poaching for bones, skin, and organs remains a threat. TRAFFIC India estimates one tiger killed every 4 days in India's peak poaching years (1990s). Now much reduced — 2022 poaching confirmed: 26 tigers.

Rhino: Horn worth more than gold by weight. Kaziranga deployed over 600 armed forest guards; shoot-at-sight for poachers since 2015. Poaching reduced dramatically (from 30+/year to single digits).

Pangolin: Most trafficked mammal globally. India has both the Indian and Chinese pangolin. Scales used in traditional Chinese medicine. All 8 pangolin species are CITES Appendix I listed.

Star Tortoise, Monitor Lizard, Tokay Gecko: Supplied to international pet and traditional medicine trade. Major seizures at Indian airports (Chennai, Delhi, Mumbai) routinely.
Initiatives Project Tiger · Project Elephant · Wetlands · International Treaties India's conservation policy architecture
Legal Framework: India's wildlife is protected under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (amended 2002, 2022), Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980. India is signatory to CITES, Ramsar Convention, Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), and the Paris Climate Agreement with NDC commitments relevant to forest cover.
Project Tiger
Launched: 1973 | NTCA established: 2005
India's most successful conservation programme. Launched by PM Indira Gandhi in 1973 with 9 reserves and ~1,800 tigers. Today: 55 Tiger Reserves across 18 states covering 78,135 km². Tiger population — 1,827 (1972) → 3,695 (2022) — a 100% increase.

National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) oversees all tiger reserves. Mandated tiger census every 4 years using camera traps, pug marks, and individual identification (stripe patterns). India accounts for 75% of world's wild tigers.

M-STRIPES: Monitoring System for Tigers — Intensive Protection and Ecological Status. App-based field data collection by forest guards. Used in all 55 tiger reserves.

Recent success stories: Panna (MP) — tigers went locally extinct in 2009, reintroduced, now has 70+. Satkosia (Odisha) — recent successful establishment. Dudhwa — rhinos reintroduced alongside tigers.
Active — Flagship programme
Project Elephant
Launched: 1992
Centrally Sponsored Scheme to protect elephants, their habitat, and corridors. 33 Elephant Reserves notified. India has ~27,000–30,000 wild Asian elephants — 60% of Asia's total. Key activities: corridor protection, anti-poaching, veterinary support for conflict-injured elephants, HWC mitigation.

Gaj Yatra: 2017–18 campaign raising awareness for elephant corridors. Haathi App: GPS tracking of GPS-collared wild elephants with alert system for villages. Elephant Task Force Report (2010): Landmark document recommending 88 elephant corridors — 22 identified as priority.
Active
Project Cheetah — Reintroduction
Launched: September 17, 2022
World's first inter-continental cheetah translocation. 8 cheetahs from Namibia released at Kuno National Park (MP) in September 2022, followed by 12 from South Africa in February 2023. Total: 20 cheetahs, of which 8 have died as of 2024 (disease, radio collar injuries, territory fights). Surviving animals have produced cubs.

Asiatic cheetahs were declared extinct in India in 1952. African cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) are a closely related subspecies selected due to genetic compatibility of habitat. Long-term goal: restore grassland ecology by re-establishing an apex predator in Kuno, Gandisagar (MP), Mukundra (Rajasthan), and Gandhi Sagar ecosystems.
Ongoing — Results mixed
Project Crocodile / Gharial Conservation
Launched: 1975
India had three native crocodilian species — mugger, saltwater crocodile, and gharial. Gharial (Gavialis gangeticus) was critically endangered (population ~250 in 1970s) due to hunting and river modification. National Chambal Sanctuary (UP/MP/Rajasthan) is the last stronghold — a shared tri-state protected river stretch.

Captive breeding at Kukrail (Lucknow), Deori, and Tikarpada — over 4,000 released into Chambal. Current wild population: ~650–900. Threats remain: sand mining, fishing nets, river dams. Project Mugger and Saltwater Crocodile (Bhitarkanika) — saltwater croc recovered from 96 individuals (1975) to 1,700+ today.
Active
Sea Turtle Conservation Programme
Ongoing since 1980s
Gahirmatha Marine Sanctuary (Odisha): World's largest olive ridley rookery. Declared Marine Sanctuary in 1997. Fishing trawlers banned within 20 km during nesting season. Odisha Marine Fisheries Regulation Act mandates Turtle Excluder Devices (TEDs) in trawl nets.

Forest Department Hatcheries: Eggs relocated from vulnerable beaches to hatcheries at Rushikulya, Devi, and Gahirmatha. 200,000+ hatchlings released annually. Satellite tagging programme reveals migratory routes — tagged turtles traced to Thailand, Sri Lanka, and Andaman.
Active
Wetland Conservation — Ramsar Sites
India has 82 Ramsar Sites (2024) — World's 3rd largest
India became the country with the most Ramsar-designated wetlands in the world (by number) in 2022. Key sites: Chilika Lake (Odisha) — 1,100 km², largest coastal wetland in Asia; Keoladeo Ghana NP (Rajasthan); Loktak Lake (Manipur) — only floating national park (Keibul Lamjao, home of sangai — Manipur's state animal, the brow-antlered deer).

National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Ecosystems (NPCA): Restoration of 150+ lakes and wetlands. Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017 — legal framework for regulation of activities in wetland buffers.
Expanded — 82 Ramsar sites
CAMPA — Compensatory Afforestation
CAMPA Act 2016
When forest land is diverted for development (mines, roads, projects), the project developer must pay into the Compensatory Afforestation Fund and plant double the area in non-forest land or degrade forest area. CAMPA has accumulated over ₹65,000 crore (2024). Critics argue compensatory plantation doesn't replace old-growth forest biodiversity. However, CAMPA funds are now being directed to wildlife habitat improvement, anti-poaching, and soil & water conservation.
Active
Snow Leopard Conservation — SECURE Himalaya
UNDP-GEF Project: 2019–2024
SECURE Himalaya (Snow Leopard Ecosystem Conservation and Recovery) — implemented across Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, and Arunachal Pradesh. India became the first country to release a National Action Plan for Snow Leopard (NAPSL) in 2019. Camera trap census in 2023 estimated India's snow leopard population at 718 — higher than earlier estimates.

Community-based livestock insurance schemes (Himachal, Uttarakhand) to reduce retaliatory killing. Predator-proof corrals provided to herder communities. Rangers trained in camera trap deployment.

Chilika Lake
Odisha · 1,165 km² · Asia's Largest Coastal Lagoon
India's first Ramsar Site (1981). A shallow brackish-water lagoon separated from the Bay of Bengal by a sand bar. Fed by 52 rivers and rivulets. Hosts 160+ bird species in winter — flamingo, pelican, bar-headed goose. Habitat for the endangered Irrawaddy dolphin (India's only population in a lagoon). Chilika Development Authority restored the lake's ecology after severe hyacinth growth and silting — one of Asia's great wetland restoration stories.

Threats: Prawn aquaculture encroachment, siltation from Mahanadi, tourism pressure.

Loktak Lake
Manipur · 287 km² · World's Only Floating National Park
The largest freshwater lake in Northeast India. Famous for phumdis — heterogeneous masses of vegetation, soil, and organic matter floating on the lake surface. The Keibul Lamjao National Park (40 km²) sits on the largest phumdi — the only floating national park in the world, home to the Sangai (brow-antlered deer, Manipur's state animal — only ~260 individuals survive).

Sundarbans
West Bengal · ~9,630 km² India side
World's largest mangrove wetland and delta. Formed by Ganga–Brahmaputra–Meghna confluence. Ramsar 2001, UNESCO WHS 1987. India's Ramsar Sites — Regional Distribution

Wetland Threats
Encroachment: Over 33% of India's wetlands have been lost since 1970 to agriculture, urbanisation, and aquaculture. Urban wetlands (Delhi's Bhalswa, Mumbai's Bhandup) shrink every decade despite legal protection.

Pollution: Industrial effluents, agricultural runoff (fertilisers, pesticides), and untreated sewage are the primary killers of freshwater wetland biodiversity. The Ganga, Yamuna, and Sabarmati carry some of the highest biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) loads in the world.

Invasive species: Water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) covers millions of hectares of Indian lakes — Loktak, Chilika (before restoration), Kerala backwaters. Blocks sunlight, depletes oxygen, destroys fish habitats.

Sand and gravel mining: Riverbed mining destroys nesting banks of gharials, river terns, skimmers and habitat of freshwater dolphins in Chambal, Ganga, Ken, Betwa rivers.
Wetland Conservation Programmes
NPCA (National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Ecosystems): Centrally sponsored scheme for integrated conservation and management of wetlands including lakes. 150+ wetlands under active restoration (e.g., Dal Lake, Loktak, Chilika).

Wetlands (Conservation & Management) Rules 2017: Prohibit draining, reclaiming, construction, or solid waste dumping within wetland boundaries. District-level Wetland Authority for each state.

Namami Gange Mission: ₹20,000 crore programme for Ganga river rejuvenation — sewage treatment plants, riverfront development, afforestation. Gangetic dolphin numbers recovering in treated stretches.

Project Dolphin (2020): Analogous to Project Tiger — dedicated programme for Gangetic and Oceanic dolphins. Census, anti-poaching, river corridor protection. National Dolphin Research Centre at Patna University.
