Chapter 01

PLATE TECTONICS

Theory of continental drift, plate boundaries & their consequences

Core Idea: The Earth's lithosphere is broken into ~15 major tectonic plates that float on the semi-molten asthenosphere and move via convection currents. Alfred Wegener's Continental Drift (1912) was the precursor; Harry Hess's Sea-floor Spreading (1960s) completed the picture.
Lithosphere: crust + upper mantle
Asthenosphere: plastic-like layer beneath
Plates move: 2–15 cm/year
Major plates: 7 major + ~8 minor
Figure 1 — Three Types of Plate Boundaries
🔀

Divergent Boundaries

  • Plates move apart
  • Magma rises, creates new crust
  • Forms mid-ocean ridges & rift valleys
  • Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift
  • Shallow-focus earthquakes, basaltic volcanism
🏔️

Convergent Boundaries

  • Plates collide — three sub-types:
  • Ocean-Ocean: Island arcs, deep trenches (Japan)
  • Ocean-Continent: Subduction, Andes-type mountains
  • Continent-Continent: Fold mountains (Himalayas)
  • Deep-focus quakes, andesitic volcanoes
↔️

Transform Boundaries

  • Plates slide horizontally past each other
  • No crust created or destroyed
  • Forms strike-slip faults
  • Example: San Andreas Fault (California)
  • Shallow-focus, high-magnitude earthquakes

Wegener's Evidence

Continental Drift Theory — Key Evidence

  • Jig-saw fit: Coastlines of South America & Africa match perfectly
  • Fossil similarity: Glossopteris flora found across Gondwana continents
  • Rock correlation: Matching geological strata across separated continents
  • Palaeoclimatic evidence: Glacial deposits in tropical regions (ancient ice sheets)
  • Sea-floor spreading (Hess): Magnetic striping on ocean floor confirmed plate movement

Chapter 02

EARTHQUAKES

Seismology — waves, measurement & effects

📍

Focus / Hypocentre

The point within the Earth where the earthquake originates. Also called the hypocentre. Energy is released here as seismic waves radiating outward.

🎯

Epicentre

The point on the Earth's surface directly above the focus. Maximum destruction occurs here. Distance from focus to epicentre = focal depth.

📏

Focal Depth

  • Shallow: 0–70 km (most destructive)
  • Intermediate: 70–300 km
  • Deep: 300–700 km
Figure 2 — Seismic Wave Types & Earth Interior Paths

📊

Richter Scale

  • Logarithmic scale: each unit = 10× amplitude
  • Each unit ≈ 31.6× energy
  • <2.0: Micro (not felt)
  • 2.0–3.9: Minor
  • 4.0–4.9: Light
  • 5.0–5.9: Moderate
  • 6.0–6.9: Strong
  • 7.0–7.9: Major
  • ≥8.0: Great
  • Modern: Moment Magnitude Scale (Mw) preferred
🏚️

Modified Mercalli Scale

  • I: Not felt
  • II–III: Felt by few, delicate objects sway
  • IV–V: Felt indoors, dishes rattle, sleepers wake
  • VI–VII: Felt by all, some structural damage
  • VIII: Considerable damage, chimneys fall
  • IX–X: Great damage, buildings destroyed
  • XI–XII: Few structures survive, total destruction
🌊

Tsunami

  • Triggered by submarine earthquakes (Mw ≥7.0) displacing water
  • Speed in deep ocean: up to 800 km/h
  • Wavelength: hundreds of km, height increases near shore
  • 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami: Sumatra (Mw 9.1) → 14 countries
  • Warning system: DART buoys + INCOIS (India)
UPSC Trick: Richter = energy at SOURCE (one value). Mercalli = EFFECT at location (varies with distance). Shadow Zone = 103°–142° from epicentre (P waves refracted, S waves blocked by liquid outer core).

INDIA SEISMIC ZONES

BIS Zoning map — Zone II to Zone V

Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) divides India into 4 seismic zones (II–V) based on past seismicity, geology, and tectonic setup. About 59% of India's land area is vulnerable to earthquakes of moderate-to-severe intensity.

Major Indian Earthquakes (UPSC-relevant)

🏚️

Bhuj 2001

State: Gujarat | Magnitude: 7.7 Mw
Killed ~20,000. Triggered policy for National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA). Zone V — Kutch.

🏚️

Latur 1993

State: Maharashtra | Magnitude: 6.2 Mw
Killed ~9,748. Shallow intraplate earthquake — surprised experts since Deccan plateau thought to be stable.

🏚️

Uttarkashi 1991 & Chamoli 1999

State: Uttarakhand | Zone V Himalayan belt. Himalayan seismicity driven by Indo-Eurasian plate collision at 5 cm/year.

🌊

Andaman 2004

Mw: 9.1 | Triggered Indian Ocean Tsunami. Andaman-Nicobar in Zone V. Led to establishment of INCOIS early warning system.

Remember: India is sandwiched between the Eurasian and Indo-Australian plates. The Himalayas are still rising due to ongoing subduction (~5 mm/year). The entire northern belt (Himalayas + NE India) is Zone IV–V. Stable Peninsular India (Deccan Craton) is mostly Zone II–III — but intraplate earthquakes DO occur (Latur, Koyna).
Chapter 04

VOLCANOES

Types, structures, distribution & volcanic landforms

Figure 4 — Composite Volcano Cross-Section & Intrusive Features

Types of Volcanoes

🛡️

Shield Volcano

  • Broad, gently sloping, dome-shaped
  • Low-viscosity basaltic lava — flows far
  • Quiet, non-explosive eruptions
  • Largest by volume on Earth
  • Example: Mauna Loa, Mauna Kea (Hawaii)
  • Found at hotspots & divergent boundaries
🗻

Composite / Stratovolcano

  • Steep, symmetrical cone with alternating ash & lava layers
  • High-viscosity andesitic/rhyolitic lava
  • Explosive, violent eruptions (pyroclastic flows)
  • Examples: Mt Fuji, Mt Vesuvius, Mt St Helens
  • Found at convergent (subduction) zones
  • Ring of Fire type
🪨

Cinder Cone

  • Simplest, smallest volcano type
  • Built from pyroclastic fragments (cinders)
  • Steep sides, bowl-shaped crater
  • Short-lived, single eruptive period
  • Example: Paricutin (Mexico, appeared 1943)
  • Often parasitic cones on shield/composite

Hotspots

Mantle Hotspots — Stationary Heat Plumes

  • Mantle plumes — columns of super-heated mantle material rising from deep inside Earth
  • Hotspot remains stationary while tectonic plate moves over it → creates chain of volcanic islands
  • Hawaiian Chain: Classic example — older islands to NW, active Kilauea/Mauna Loa to SE. Traces Pacific Plate direction
  • Yellowstone: Continental hotspot — supervolcano beneath Wyoming
  • Iceland: Hotspot + divergent boundary (Mid-Atlantic Ridge)
  • Reunion Island: Hotspot responsible for Deccan Traps flood basalt (66 Ma)
  • ~About 40–50 known hotspots globally

Volcanic Activity Status

🔴

Active

Currently erupting or showing signs (gas emission, seismicity). ~1,500 potentially active. Examples: Kilauea (Hawaii), Etna (Italy), Barren Island (India)

🟡

Dormant

Not currently active but may erupt again. Long quiescent periods. Examples: Mt Rainier (USA), Taal (Philippines, erupted 2020)

🟢

Extinct

No activity expected; magma supply cut off. Examples: Ben Nevis (UK), Edinburgh Castle rock, Deccan Traps vents

Caldera: Large depression formed when a volcano collapses after a massive eruption emptying the magma chamber. NOT just a large crater. Examples: Crater Lake (Oregon), Yellowstone caldera, Lake Toba (Sumatra — supereruption 74,000 years ago).
Chapter 05

RING OF FIRE

The Pacific horseshoe — seismicity, volcanism & plate tectonics

Figure 5 — Ring of Fire Schematic (Pacific Basin)
Length: ~40,000 km
Earthquakes: ~90% of world's total
Volcanoes: ~75% of world's active
Deepest trench: Mariana (~11,034 m)
Shape: Horseshoe
🌏

Why So Active?

The Pacific Plate is surrounded by convergent and transform boundaries. The Pacific Plate subducts under surrounding plates, creating deep trenches, fold mountains, and volcanic arcs around the entire margin. Subducting oceanic crust melts in asthenosphere, producing andesitic magma that rises to form composite volcanoes.

🗺️

Major Trenches & Arcs

  • Mariana Trench: 11,034 m — Pacific + Philippine plates
  • Japan Trench: Pacific subducts under Eurasian
  • Peru-Chile Trench: Nazca subducts under S. America
  • Aleutian Trench: Pacific subducts under N. American
  • Java Trench: Indo-Australian subducts under Eurasian
💥

Historic Catastrophic Events

  • Krakatau 1883: VEI 6; tsunamis killed 36,000
  • Mt Pinatubo 1991: Largest 20th-c eruption
  • Mt St Helens 1980: Lateral blast, 57 deaths
  • Tohoku 2011: Mw 9.0, tsunami, Fukushima
  • Good Friday 1964: Alaska Mw 9.2
UPSC Note: India is NOT part of the Ring of Fire. India lies on the Indo-Australian plate. The Andaman & Nicobar Islands, however, are on the eastern margin of the Indian plate near the Sunda subduction zone — hence seismically active. Barren Island (India's only active volcano) is in this zone.
Chapter 06

KEY EXAMPLES

Deccan Traps, Barren Island & UPSC-relevant case studies

Deccan Traps

🌋

Formation

  • Formed ~66 million years ago (Late Cretaceous)
  • Massive flood basalt province — fissure/effusive eruption
  • Original area: ~1.5 million km² (now ~500,000 km² exposed)
  • Thickness: up to 2,000 m in places
  • Caused by Reunion Island hotspot as India moved over it
🦕

Significance

  • Coincided with K-Pg mass extinction (dinosaur extinction)
  • Debate: volcanic winters from SO₂ vs Chicxulub asteroid
  • Created the Deccan Plateau / Deccan lava plateau
  • Rich in black cotton soil (Regur soil)
  • Important for agriculture — Maharashtra, MP, Karnataka
  • Intrusive features: Dykes, Sills visible in Sahyadri ranges
⛏️

Economic Importance

  • Black cotton soil (Regur) — ideal for cotton cultivation
  • Basaltic rocks — quarried for construction
  • Mineral resources — manganese, iron ore
  • Diamonds found in kimberlite pipes of Deccan region
  • Grape cultivation (viticulture) in Nashik on basaltic soils

Barren Island

India's Only Active Volcano

  • Location: Andaman Sea, ~135 km NE of Port Blair, Andaman & Nicobar Islands
  • Type: Stratovolcano (composite) — andesitic lava
  • Tectonic setting: Near Sunda Subduction Zone (Burma plate subducting under Indo-Australian plate)
  • First recorded eruption: 1787 (by Archibald Blair)
  • Dormant period: ~150 years (1832–1991)
  • Recent activity: Active phases 1991, 1994–95, 2005–06, 2008–09, 2017, 2020
  • UPSC angle: Only active volcano in South Asia; uninhabited island under Indian jurisdiction

Narcondam Island

India's Only Dormant Volcano

  • Located NE of Barren Island in Andaman Sea
  • Dormant stratovolcano; last eruption unknown (prehistoric)
  • Home to Narcondam hornbill (endemic species)
  • Wildlife sanctuary; no permanent human habitation

Notable Global Comparisons

🏔️

Himalayas vs Andes

  • Himalayas: Continent-continent collision (India + Eurasian plates). No subduction, no volcanism. Only fold mountains + earthquakes.
  • Andes: Ocean-continent collision (Nazca subducting under S. America). Active volcanoes + earthquakes + fold mountains.
🌊

Mid-Ocean Ridges

  • Mid-Atlantic Ridge: Longest mountain range on Earth (~80,000 km); divergent boundary
  • Iceland: sits on Mid-Atlantic Ridge + hotspot
  • Sea-floor spreading creates new oceanic crust
  • Oldest ocean floor subducted at trenches — cycle continues
⛰️

World's Famous Volcanoes

  • Mauna Kea (Hawaii): Tallest from ocean floor (10,210 m)
  • Mt Etna (Sicily): Most active in Europe
  • Vesuvius: Destroyed Pompeii 79 CE
  • Krakatau (Indonesia): 1883 — heard 5,000 km away
  • Lake Toba (Sumatra): Super-eruption 74,000 ya — genetic bottleneck theory

Answer
INCOIS (Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services), Hyderabad, operates India's Tsunami Early Warning Centre (ITEWC).

Established post-2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami. Uses DART (Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis) buoys, bottom pressure recorders, tide gauges, and seismic data to issue warnings within 10–20 minutes of an earthquake. UPSC CSE Prelims 2014
Which of the following is/are the possible consequence(s) of heavy sand mining in riverbeds?

[Extended concept] — What is 'Shadow Zone' in seismology?
Tap to reveal answer
Answer
Shadow Zone = region on Earth's surface (between 103° and 142° from the epicentre) where neither P nor S waves are received from an earthquake.

• P wave shadow zone: 103°–180° (P waves refracted by core)
• S wave shadow zone: 103°–180° (S cannot travel through liquid outer core)
• Complete shadow (no P or S): 103°–142°

This is how scientists determined the outer core is liquid (S waves cannot pass through liquids).
UPSC CSE Mains GS-1 2021
Discuss the causes of depletion of mangroves and explain their importance in maintaining coastal ecology. (Linked topic) — How does a tsunami differ from a storm surge? Explain the geological conditions that generate a tsunami.
Tap to reveal answer
Key Points
Tsunami vs Storm Surge:
Tsunami — triggered by submarine earthquake/landslide/volcanic eruption; long wavelength (100–500 km); travels at 800 km/h in deep ocean; height increases near shore (shoaling).
Storm Surge — driven by wind and pressure of tropical cyclone; localized; different physics.

Geological conditions for tsunami: Subduction zone earthquake (Mw ≥7.0); vertical displacement of seafloor; shallow focal depth (<70 km); enough energy to displace massive water column. Classic: Sumatra 2004 (Indian plate + Burma plate boundary).
Exam Strategy: For earthquake PYQs — always check if statements mix magnitude/intensity, Richter/Mercalli, or zone details. For volcano PYQs — Barren Island (active) vs Narcondam (dormant) is a classic trap. Deccan Traps connection to K-Pg extinction and Reunion hotspot comes up repeatedly in Mains.
