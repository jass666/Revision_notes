Tab 01 — Origin & Identity
What is Jainism?
A path of total liberation through absolute non-violence and conquest of the self

Jainism is one of the world's oldest continuously practiced religions, originating in the Gangetic plains of ancient India. Its name derives from Jina ("conqueror") — one who has conquered the passions and achieved liberation. It centers on the attainment of Moksha (liberation of the soul) through ethical living, asceticism, and the three jewels of Right Faith, Right Knowledge, and Right Conduct. With approximately 4–5 million adherents today, Jainism punches far above its demographic weight in philosophy, art, commerce, and ethics.

Why did Jainism arise? The 6th century BCE was India's Axial Age — a period of radical intellectual ferment. Jainism arose as part of the Shramana movement, a broad current of wandering ascetics who rejected Vedic Brahmanical authority, the caste system's spiritual monopoly, and the violence of animal sacrifices (yajnas). Its radical insistence on ahimsa (non-violence) toward all living beings was a direct indictment of Vedic ritual killing and Kshatriya warfare culture.
The Three Jewels (Triratna)
Ratnatraya — रत्नत्रय
👁
Samyak Darshana
Right Faith — correct perception and reverence for the Jinas (enlightened beings) and the Jain truth. The foundational attitude of trust in the Jain path.
📖
Samyak Jnana
Right Knowledge — correct, undistorted understanding of the nature of the soul, the universe, and the path of liberation. Free from doubt and perversity.
🕊
Samyak Charitra
Right Conduct — ethical behaviour embodied in the Five Vows (Pancha Mahavrata for monks, Anuvrata for laity). The practical expression of Jain values.
Historical Context
6th Century BCE India

Political Landscape

The subcontinent was divided into the 16 Mahajanapadas (great republics and kingdoms). Magadha (capital Rajagriha) was rising to dominance. Vaishali, where Mahavira was born, was a flourishing democratic republic of the Vrijji confederacy — a cosmopolitan centre of trade and ideas.

Social Tensions

The varna system had calcified. Kshatriyas and Vaishyas — warriors and merchants — resented Brahmin supremacy in ritual and social life. Jainism appealed particularly to the Vaishya merchant class, offering a spiritual path that valued non-violence (compatible with trade, not animal husbandry or warfare) and rejected caste as a spiritual determinant.

Shramana Movement

Jainism claims tremendous antiquity — Mahavira was the 24th Tirthankara, not the founder. The 23rd, Parshvanatha (c. 9th–8th century BCE), is historically attested; his followers were already established when Mahavira was born. Jainism thus predates Buddhism in institutional form, even if both reached maturity simultaneously.

Jain Critique of Vedic Orthodoxy

Jainism rejected: Vedic authority, Brahmin caste privilege, animal sacrifice (himsa), the concept of a creator God (Jainism is atheistic — anishvara-vada), and the idea that karma is merely ritual action. Jain karma is a physical substance that literally sticks to the soul — a unique metaphysical claim.

Jainism's Unique Position: Unlike Buddhism, Jainism asserts a permanent, individual soul (jiva) that is distinct from matter (ajiva). Unlike Vedanta, the soul is not Brahman — each soul is unique and independent. Unlike theistic traditions, there is no creator God. The Tirthankaras are perfected humans who achieved liberation — models to emulate, not gods to pray to.
The Shramana Tradition
Buddhism
Founded by Siddhartha Gautama (contemporary of Mahavira). Middle Path, impermanence, no-self (anatta). Rejected extreme asceticism. Shared critique of Brahminism.
Ajivikas
Founded by Makkhali Gosala (a former disciple of Mahavira). Absolute determinism (niyati) — all beings are fixed on a cosmic wheel of exactly 8.4 million great eons of rebirth.
Charvaka / Lokayata
Indian materialism. Denied afterlife, soul, karma, ahimsa obligations. "Eat, drink, and be merry." Direct perception as the only valid pramana (source of knowledge).
Tab 02 — Life & Legend
The Life of Vardhamana Mahavira
Prince, ascetic, conqueror of the self — the 24th and last Tirthankara

Vardhamana — later known as Mahavira ("Great Hero") — was born c. 599 BCE (some traditions say 540 BCE) in Kundagrama (Vaishali, modern Bihar) to King Siddhartha of the Jnatrika Kshatriya clan and Queen Trishala. Astrological omens and prophetic dreams at his birth — the Fourteen Great Dreams — foretold that he would become either a world-emperor (chakravartin) or a great spiritual liberator (Tirthankara).

The Great Renunciation
Diksha — Initiation into Asceticism

At age 30, after the death of his parents (who undertook the Jain rite of santhara — fasting unto death), Mahavira sought and received permission from the Jnatrika clan council to renounce worldly life. He donated all his possessions, pulled out his hair in five handfuls (panchashti — a Jain initiation rite still practiced today), and became a wandering naked ascetic. He abandoned all clothing — unlike the monks of Parshvanatha, who wore a single white cloth.

He initially wandered for 13 months with another monk, then chose complete solitude — no companion, no shelter, no possessions, no speech unless necessary. He endured extraordinary hardships: village dogs bit him, children pelted him with stones, farmers tied a yoke to his neck.
Twelve Years of Austerity
Sadhana — The Path of Hardship

For 12 years and 6 months, Mahavira practiced the most extreme asceticism in ancient Indian history. He observed long fasts (sometimes up to 6 months of minimal eating). He endured heat, cold, insects, and violence without reaction. He practiced total non-violence — moving carefully to avoid harming even microscopic beings. He meditated in forests, cremation grounds, and abandoned houses.

Unlike the Buddha, Mahavira did not abandon extreme asceticism — he saw it as the necessary burning away of accumulated karma. The body's suffering was the soul's purification. This is the fundamental difference between Jain and Buddhist methods.
The Enlightenment
Kevala Jnana — Omniscience

📍 Jrimbhikagrama, Bihar · c. 557 BCE

On the bank of the Rijuvalika River, under a sala tree, Mahavira attained Kevala Jnana — perfect, omniscient, infinite knowledge encompassing all things in all times. Unlike the Buddha's Bodhi (awakening from ignorance), Kevala Jnana in Jainism means the soul is fully freed from the obscuring karmas that had blocked its infinite, natural capacity for knowledge.

Precondition
Kshapaka Shreni
The soul climbs the ladder of spiritual purity (gunasthana), burning away the four "knowledge-obscuring" karmas through meditation and austerity.
The Moment
Kevala Jnana
Infinite knowledge, infinite perception (darshana), infinite bliss, and infinite energy simultaneously manifest. Mahavira becomes a Jina — a conqueror. The 24th Tirthankara.
After
30 Years of Teaching
Mahavira taught for 30 years, establishing the fourfold Jain Sangha: monks (sadhus), nuns (sadhvis), laymen (shravaka), laywomen (shravika).

Key Patrons & Disciples

King Shrenik (Bimbisara) of Magadha was a devoted lay follower. His son Ajatashatru also supported Jainism. The merchant community — especially in Magadha, Anga, and Videha — formed Jainism's most loyal lay patrons. Mahavira's 11 chief disciples (Ganadharas) included Indrabhuti Gautama, Agnibhuti, and Vayubhuti.

Nirvana · 527 BCE

Mahavira attained final liberation (Moksha / Nirvana) at Pavapuri (modern Pawapuri, Bihar) at age 72. He died in the Jain manner — complete fasting (santhara or sallekhana). His death is celebrated as Diwali in the Jain tradition — the festival of lights marks the "light of knowledge" that was extinguished from the world at his passing.

Mahavira vs. the Buddha — A Critical Comparison: Both were Kshatriya princes; both renounced the world at age 29–30; both lived in the same region at the same time (they may have met, though no confirmed record exists); both rejected Brahmin authority and caste. But Mahavira embraced extreme asceticism while Buddha rejected it. Mahavira affirmed a permanent soul (jiva); Buddha denied a permanent self (anatta). Mahavira's karma is physical substance; Buddha's karma is mental intention.
The 24 Tirthankaras
The Ford-Makers

The Tirthankaras are the 24 liberated beings who, in each cosmic cycle, re-establish the Jain path for humanity. "Tirthankara" means "ford-maker" — one who creates a ford (tirtha) across the ocean of suffering.

The 24 Tirthankaras of the current cosmic cycle — select key ones shown · All attained Kevala Jnana

Rishabhadeva (Adinatha) — 1st Tirthankara

The first Tirthankara of the current cosmic cycle — a legendary figure considered by Jains to have lived in primordial times. He is mentioned in Hindu texts (Bhagavata Purana, Vishnu Purana) and even the Rigveda (some scholars argue), giving Jainism its claim to being the oldest of all Indian religions. His symbol is the bull.

Parshvanatha — 23rd Tirthankara

The most historically attested pre-Mahavira Tirthankara, traditionally dated c. 872–772 BCE (9th–8th century BCE). He taught a fourfold restraint: non-violence, non-lying, non-stealing, non-possession. Mahavira later added a 5th vow: celibacy (brahmacharya). Parshvanatha's symbol is the serpent. His followers — the Nirgrantha Shramanas — were already active when Mahavira was born.

Tab 03 — Philosophy
Core Teachings of Jainism
A complete metaphysical system — soul, matter, karma, and the path to liberation
The Five Great Vows
Pancha Mahavrata

The ethical core of Jain monastic life. Lay Jains observe the "lesser vows" (Anuvrata) — modified versions of the same five. All are in service of one supreme value: Ahimsa.

Ahimsa paramo dharma — "Non-violence is the supreme religion." Jain ahimsa is the most radical in the world. Monks carry a rajoharan (soft broom) to sweep insects from their path. They wear a mukha-patti (mouth cloth) to avoid inhaling tiny insects. They do not eat after sunset (to avoid insects attracted to lamps).


Jains classify life into 5 categories by number of senses:

• Ekendriya — 1-sense beings (plants, earth, water, fire, air bodies) — minimum harm unavoidable

• Dvindriya — 2-sense beings (worms, leeches)

• Trindriya — 3-sense beings (lice, ants)

• Chaturindriya — 4-sense beings (flies, mosquitoes)

• Panchendriya — 5-sense beings (animals, humans) — harming these is most severely condemned


UPSC Note: Jain ahimsa directly influenced Gandhi's satyagraha. Gandhi acknowledged Jainism as the most profound formulation of non-violence in human history. The Jain merchant community of Gujarat was Gandhi's early support base.

Satya means speaking only what is true, beneficial, and not harmful. In cases of conflict between truth and ahimsa — where speaking truth would lead to greater violence — ahimsa takes precedence. This reflects the deeply integrated nature of Jain ethics.


This vow is related to the epistemological principle of Anekantavada — the many-sided nature of reality. Because absolute truth about any complex matter can only be perceived from all angles simultaneously (as a Jina does), humans must speak with epistemic humility, using syat ("perhaps") qualifiers.

Monks must not take anything without explicit permission — even water, food, or shelter. Lay Jains apply this as avoiding dishonest business practices, tax evasion, and misappropriation. In broader Jain ethics, this supports a strong tradition of business integrity — Jain merchants historically maintained a reputation for scrupulous honesty in trade.

This was the 5th vow added by Mahavira to the original four of Parshvanatha. For Jain monks, it means complete sexual abstinence. The rationale: sexual activity involves violence (against microscopic beings), attachment (which binds karma), and passion (which is a primary source of karma-influx). Celibacy is thus simultaneously an ethical and a soteriological requirement.


For lay Jains, the equivalent lesser vow is fidelity within marriage and avoidance of sexual indulgence outside it.

Jain monks (Digambara) renounce all possessions including clothing. Shvetambara monks retain one white cloth and a begging bowl. For lay Jains, the equivalent vow is parigraha-parimana — limiting one's possessions to what is genuinely needed. This has produced, paradoxically, a community of extremely wealthy merchants who donate lavishly to build temples and support the Sangha.


Jain charitable institutions (panjrapoles — animal shelters; hospitals; educational institutions) represent the lay community's expression of this vow — redistributing wealth rather than accumulating it without limit.


UPSC Note: The Digambara-Shvetambara split centers primarily on this vow — whether clothing constitutes "possession" and whether women can achieve liberation without first being reborn as men.

Jain Metaphysics
The Soul, Karma & Liberation

Jainism has the most elaborately developed karma theory in any Indian tradition — and it is radically physical.

🔮 Hover a concept
Jain cosmology maps the journey of the soul from bondage to liberation through a precise physical theory of karmic matter.

The Six Dravyas (Substances)

Jain ontology recognizes six fundamental substances: Jiva (soul/living), Pudgala (matter), Dharma (principle of motion), Adharma (principle of rest), Akasha (space), and Kala (time). The universe is eternal — it was never created and will never be destroyed. There is no creator God.

Karma as Physical Matter

Unlike Buddhism (where karma = intention) or Hinduism (where karma = action's ritual/moral consequence), Jain karma consists of actual microscopic particles (pudgala) that attach to the soul through passions (kashaya). These particles literally weigh down, colour, and obscure the soul's innate infinite capacities. Liberation = complete removal of all karmic matter.

Anekantavada — Many-Sided Truth

Perhaps Jainism's most intellectually sophisticated contribution to philosophy. Reality is complex and multi-dimensional — no single perspective captures the complete truth. Two doctrines express this: Syadvada (conditional predication — "perhaps it is…") and Nayavada (doctrine of partial standpoints). Each claim is true from a certain angle (naya) but not absolutely.

The 14 Gunasthanas

The soul's journey to liberation is mapped across 14 stages of spiritual development (gunasthanas) — from total ignorance (mithyadrishti) to the state of omniscience with body (sayogi-kevalin) and finally bodiless liberation (siddha). Each stage is characterized by precise degrees of karma suppression or elimination. A uniquely systematic spiritual developmental map.

The Blind Men & the Elephant
Anekantavada — अनेकान्तवाद
The famous Jain parable: several blind men each touch a different part of an elephant and disagree on what it is — a wall (flank), a rope (tail), a tree trunk (leg). Each is partially right from their standpoint. Only the one who can see the whole elephant knows the complete truth. Anekantavada says all finite beings are like the blind men — only an omniscient Jina sees the elephant whole.
🔀
Syadvada
The doctrine of conditional predication. Every statement must be qualified with "syat" (perhaps/in some sense). "Syat asti" (in some sense, it is); "syat nasti" (in some sense, it is not). Seven-fold predication (saptabhangi) systematizes all possible qualified statements.
👁
Nayavada
The doctrine of partial standpoints (nayas). A naya is a particular perspective that illuminates one aspect of reality. Nayas include: dravyarthika (substance perspective), paryayarthika (modification perspective), and several more. All nayas together give a fuller picture.
🤫
Avaktavyata
The inexpressible — one of the seven predicates in saptabhangi. Reality in its totality transcends language. The Jina's omniscient knowledge cannot be fully conveyed in human speech. A sophisticated epistemological humility built into the system.
Tab 04 — Schisms & Schools
Jain Sects & Schools
Two major traditions divided on robes, scripture, and the liberation of women
Timeline of major Jain sects and their divergence · c. 300 BCE to present

🤍 Shvetambara ("White-Robed")

Monks wear a white cloth. Named from Sanskrit shveta (white) + ambara (cloth). Predominantly found in Gujarat and Rajasthan.

Key differences from Digambara:

• Monks retain a white cloth and begging bowl
• Women can attain moksha in a female body
• Accept the canonical Agama texts as authentic
• Tirthankara images shown with eyes open and clothed
• Mallibai (19th Tirthankara) was a woman (disputed by Digambaras)

Major sub-sects: Sthanakvasi, Terapanthi, Murtipujaka

◼ Digambara ("Sky-Clad")

Monks go completely naked — "clothed in sky." From Sanskrit dik (direction/sky) + ambara. Predominantly in Karnataka, Maharashtra, and Madhya Pradesh.

Key differences from Shvetambara:

• Complete nudity is mandatory for full liberation
• Women cannot attain moksha without first being reborn as men
• The original Agamas are lost — rely on later texts
• Tirthankara images are unclothed with downcast eyes
• The 19th Tirthankara (Mallinatha) was male

Major sub-sects: Bisapanthi, Terapanthi (Digambara variety)

Origin of the Schism

According to tradition, a severe 12-year famine struck Magadha c. 300 BCE. Bhadrabahu, a senior monk, led a group southward to Karnataka (Shravanabelagola) to survive. When they returned, the monks who had stayed behind had relaxed the nudity rule and accepted white cloth due to circumstances. The returned group refused to accept this compromise — and the Digambara-Shvetambara schism was born. The Shvetambara version disputes this narrative, saying the divergence was always present as a minor variation.

Tab 05 — Geographic Spread
The Spread of Jainism
From the Gangetic heartland to Gujarat, Karnataka, and the global diaspora
Spread of Jainism from its Magadha origins across India
🏛
Magadha & Anga (6th–4th c. BCE)
The heartland of Jainism — modern Bihar. Rajagriha (Rajgir), Vaishali, Champanagari, and Pataliputra were major Jain centres. Kings Bimbisara and Ajatashatru were lay followers. Chandragupta Maurya converted to Jainism at the end of his life.
🗿
Karnataka (3rd c. BCE onward)
The Bhadrabahu migration during the great famine established Karnataka as the Digambara heartland. Shravanabelagola — site of the massive Bahubali statue — became the most sacred Digambara pilgrimage site. Ganga, Hoysala, and Rashtrakuta kings patronized Jainism.
🕌
Gujarat & Rajasthan (Medieval)
The Shvetambara heartland. The Solanki (Chaulukya) dynasty of Gujarat was a major Jain patron. The Dilwara temples (Mt. Abu) and Palitana temple complex on Shatrunjaya Hill were built during this period — among the most magnificent religious architecture in India.
💼
Global Diaspora (Modern)
The Jain diaspora has spread to the UK, USA, Canada, East Africa, and Southeast Asia — concentrated in commerce and trade. Despite being only ~4 million globally, Jains have among the highest literacy rates, per-capita income, and charitable giving of any community in India.
Royal Patronage Timeline
c. 540–490 BCE
Bimbisara & Ajatashatru (Magadha)
Both Haryanka kings are described in Jain texts as lay followers and patrons of Mahavira, attending his discourses and supporting the Sangha.
c. 297 BCE
Chandragupta Maurya
The Mauryan empire's founder reportedly abdicated, migrated to Shravanabelagola with Bhadrabahu, and practiced the Jain rite of sallekhana (fasting unto death). Confirmed in Digambara tradition and some historical sources.
c. 4th–2nd c. BCE
Nanda & Kalinga Kingdoms
Kharvela of Kalinga (c. 1st c. BCE) — whose Hathigumpha inscription is a key historical source — was a devoted Jain patron who conducted military campaigns in Magadha partly to recover a Jain idol taken earlier.
7th–12th c. CE
Ganga, Rashtrakuta & Solanki Dynasties
The Ganga kings of Karnataka (who commissioned the Bahubali statue at Shravanabelagola) and the Solanki kings of Gujarat (who built Dilwara temples) represented the high tide of royal Jain patronage in art and architecture.
16th–17th c. CE
Mughal Period
Akbar was influenced by Jain scholars — particularly Hiravijaya Suri, whom he summoned to his court in 1583. He issued farmans banning animal slaughter during Paryushana (Jain holy season). Jahangir also showed interest in Jain philosophy.
Why didn't Jainism spread beyond India? Unlike Buddhism, Jainism never developed a missionary impulse. The extreme requirements of the monastic path (nudity, carrying no water, eating from palms) made monastic mobility nearly impossible. The lay community's tight integration with specific commercial communities also limited geographic expansion. Jainism spread primarily through the movement of Jain merchant families rather than missionary monks.
Tab 06 — Councils & Scriptures
Jain Councils & the Agamas
The preservation and loss of Mahavira's teachings across centuries
Timeline of Jain councils and scripture compilation

Venue: Pataliputra (modern Patna, Bihar) · Presider: Sthulabhadra


Context: A severe 12-year famine struck Magadha around this time. Bhadrabahu, the last monk who knew all 14 Purvas (original teachings) by heart, left for the south with a group of monks. Sthulabhadra stayed in Magadha with the remaining Sangha.


Outcome: Sthulabhadra convened a council to compile and preserve the teachings. The result was the 12 Angas — the primary Jain canonical texts. However, Sthulabhadra knew only 10 of the 14 Purvas himself; complete knowledge was with Bhadrabahu, who refused to share the last 4 with Sthulabhadra (due to a disciplinary dispute).


Significance: This is the moment Digambaras point to as the beginning of scriptural corruption — the monks who remained behind had compromised their vows (wearing cloth during the famine). Their compilation was therefore considered tainted. Shvetambaras accept these Agamas as authoritative.


UPSC Note: 1st Council = Pataliputra = Sthulabhadra = 12 Angas compilation. Contemporary with the first Buddhist schism (also at Pataliputra under Ashoka).

Venue: Vallabhi (Valabhi, modern Vala, Gujarat) · Presider: Devanandi (also called Devarddhi Ganimashramana)


Context: A second council had been held at Mathura (c. 300 CE), but the texts remained in oral form. By the 5th century CE, the monks who had memorized entire Agamas were dying — the tradition was at risk of complete loss.


Outcome: The Agamas were written down for the first time — a critical moment parallel to the Sri Lanka writing of the Theravada Tipitaka (c. 1st c. BCE). 45 texts were canonized as the Shvetambara Agama corpus:

• 12 Angas (primary texts)

• 12 Upangas (secondary texts)

• 10 Prakirnakas (miscellaneous texts)

• 6 Chedasutras (monastic discipline)

• 4 Mula-sutras (basic texts)

• 1 Nandi-sutra and 1 Anuyogadvaras (auxiliary)


Digambara rejection: Digambaras reject all these texts as inauthentic. They hold that the original Purvas and Angas are entirely lost, and they rely on later texts — Shatkhandagama and Kasayapahuda — compiled by Pushpadanta and Bhutabali (c. 1st–2nd century CE).


UPSC Note: 2nd Council = Vallabhi = Devanandi = written Agamas. This is the event that cemented the Shvetambara scriptural canon.

Key Scriptures
📚
Acharanga Sutra
The oldest Jain text in the Agama canon — describes monastic rules and Mahavira's life in archaic Ardhamagadhi Prakrit. Deeply important as a source for the history of Jain asceticism.
📜
Tattvartha Sutra
By Umasvati (c. 2nd–5th century CE) — the only text accepted by both Digambara and Shvetambara as authoritative. The most systematic exposition of Jain philosophy in Sanskrit. The "Bible of Jainism."
🏛
Shatkhandagama
The primary Digambara canonical text — compiled by Pushpadanta and Bhutabali (c. 1st–2nd century CE). Six-volume work on Jain cosmology, karma theory, and the nature of the soul.
📖
Kalpasutra
Shvetambara text by Bhadrabahu — contains biographies of the last four Tirthankaras (especially Mahavira) and is ritually recited during Paryushana. Key source for Mahavira's life history.
Tab 07 — Art & Architecture
Jain Art & Architecture
Among the most exquisite religious art in the world — in marble, bronze, and carved stone
The Jain Temple
Derasar & Basadi

Jain temples (derasar in Shvetambara terminology, basadi in Digambara) are among the finest examples of Indian temple architecture. The Dilwara temples at Abu Road, Rajasthan (11th–13th century CE) are considered among the most beautiful buildings in the world — their white marble carved to lace-like intricacy.

The Ranakpur Chaumukha Temple (15th century CE, Rajasthan) features 1,444 intricately carved marble pillars — no two alike. The Palitana temple complex (Shatrunjaya Hill, Gujarat) contains over 900 temples on a single hill — the largest temple complex in the world.

Iconic Monuments
🗿
Gommateshvara / Bahubali
At Shravanabelagola, Karnataka — a monolithic granite statue 57 feet tall, erected c. 981 CE by the Ganga king Chamundaraya. The world's largest free-standing stone statue at the time. Mahāmastakābhiṣeka (head-anointment ceremony) held every 12 years.
🏛
Dilwara Temples, Mt. Abu
Five marble temples built by Solanki-era merchant ministers (11th–13th century CE). Vimala Vasahi (1031 CE) and Luna Vasahi (1230 CE) are the most famous — their ceilings carved with flowers, figures, and geometric patterns to an almost unbelievable delicacy.
⛰
Palitana, Gujarat
Shatrunjaya Hill — the holiest Shvetambara pilgrimage site. 900+ temples built between the 11th and 19th centuries. Pilgrims climb 3,700 steps. Dying on this hill is considered auspicious. In 2014, the city of Palitana declared itself vegetarian — the first city in the world to do so by law.
🎨
Jain Manuscript Paintings
An extraordinary tradition of illustrated manuscripts — particularly the Kalpasutra and Kalakacharya Katha. The Shvetambara style (12th–16th century CE) is distinctive: flat, angular figures, rich gold backgrounds, extended "projecting eye" (a eye extends beyond the profile face) — a uniquely Jain artistic convention.
Tirthankara Iconography
How to Identify a Jina Image

Shvetambara Style

• Eyes open, slightly smiling
• Clothed in simple white garment
• Decorated with jewelry and ornaments
• Seated or standing with attendants (Yaksha/Yakshi)
• Each Tirthankara identified by their lanchana (symbol): bull (Rishabha), crescent (Neminatha), serpent (Parshva), lion (Mahavira)

Digambara Style

• Eyes half-closed, meditating
• Completely naked — no clothing or ornaments
• Serene, otherworldly expression
• Elongated earlobes (from heavy earrings worn as a prince, now empty)
• Arms hanging straight down (kayotsarga — body abandonment posture)
• Same lanchana system for identification
