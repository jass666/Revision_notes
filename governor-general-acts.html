Two Eras, One Thread: British legislative control of India divides cleanly into two eras — the East India Company period (1773–1858) where Parliament progressively curtailed Company autonomy, and the Crown Rule period (1858–1947) where the British government administered India directly through the Secretary of State and Viceroy. Every major act reduced Indian self-governance while the reform acts, ironically, slowly restored it.
Era Breakdown
🏴 Company Era · 1773–1858
The East India Company ruled India commercially until 1858. Parliament passed acts to regulate the Company — Regulating Act (1773), Pitt's India Act (1784), Charter Acts of 1793, 1813, 1833, and 1853. Key themes: centralising power in a single Governor-General, ending Company trade monopoly, opening India to missionaries, abolishing sati. The 1857 Revolt ended this era permanently.
👑 Crown Era · 1858–1947
After the 1857 mutiny, the Crown assumed direct control. The Government of India Act 1858 abolished the Company and created the Viceroy post. Subsequent acts oscillated between repression (IPC, Arms Act, Sedition Laws) and reform (Indian Councils Acts, Rowlatt Act protests, Morley-Minto, Montagu-Chelmsford, GOI Act 1935). The arc ended with the Indian Independence Act 1947.

Company Era 1773–1857 Parliament tightens its grip on the East India Company
Context: The British East India Company (EIC) had transformed from a trading outfit to a de facto government controlling vast swaths of India. By 1773, the Company's mismanagement, corruption (notably Warren Hastings' Bengal famine of 1770), and military overextension alarmed the British Parliament. The Charter Acts series progressively stripped the Company of commercial and political autonomy until the 1857 Revolt ended its existence entirely.
1773
Regulating Act of 1773
Warren Hastings · First Governor-General of Bengal
Parliament's first major intervention in EIC affairs. Created the post of Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings being the first). Established a Supreme Court at Calcutta with a Chief Justice and three judges. Created a four-member Executive Council — the GG could be overruled 3-to-1. Prohibited Company servants from private trade and accepting gifts.

Key provisions: GG appointed for 5 years; subordinated Bombay and Madras presidencies to Bengal; Secretary of State to oversee; Court of Directors required to report revenues to British government.
EIC Reform Supreme Court First Act
Landmark
1781
Amending Act of 1781 (Settlement Act)
Warren Hastings
Passed to rectify defects of the Regulating Act. Exempted actions of GG and Council from Supreme Court jurisdiction when done in official capacity. Established that the Supreme Court had no jurisdiction over the revenue collection. Indians could be tried by their own personal law in personal matters.

Significance: First attempt to define the boundary between civil/executive and judicial functions under British Indian law.
Judicial Clarity Admin Reform
1784
Pitt's India Act (India Act 1784)
Warren Hastings / Lord Cornwallis
Named after PM William Pitt the Younger. Established dual government: the Company retained commercial functions; a new Board of Control (six members, two cabinet ministers) supervised political, military and revenue affairs. The Board's secret committee could issue secret orders directly to India bypassing the Court of Directors.

Impact: Gave Parliament effective day-to-day control. Prohibited aggressive wars and subsidiary alliances without home approval. Distinguished commercial from political functions for the first time.
Dual Govt Board of Control Constitutional
Landmark
1793
Charter Act of 1793
Lord Cornwallis
Renewed the Company's charter for 20 years. Made the GG's powers supreme — he could overrule his council in any matter. Introduced the principle of covenanted civil service exclusively for Europeans. Provided salary from Indian revenues for the Board of Control members (controversial). Gave the GG command over Bombay and Madras military.

Also notable: Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793) under this era — Lord Cornwallis fixed land revenue permanently with zamindars.
Civil Service Charter Renewal
1813
Charter Act of 1813
Lord Minto I
Charter renewed for 20 more years but the EIC's trade monopoly over India was abolished — opening Indian trade to all British merchants. The monopoly over China tea trade and trade with China was retained until 1833. Christian missionaries were allowed to enter India for the first time. Introduced a provision for the education of Indians — ₹1 lakh annual spend on Indian education.

Significance: The beginning of the end of the Company as a trading entity; purely an administrative arm henceforth.
Monopoly Ended Education Fund Missionaries
1829
Bengal Sati Regulation (Reg. XVII)
Lord William Bentinck
Abolished the practice of sati (widow immolation) throughout the Bengal Presidency — later extended to Bombay and Madras. Passed under intense lobbying by Raja Ram Mohan Roy and against fierce orthodox Hindu opposition. First major legislative intervention in Indian social/religious practices.

Impact: Landmark in social reform; established the precedent that British administration would intervene in practices deemed inhumane regardless of religious sanction.
Social Reform Humanitarian
Social Landmark
1833
Charter Act of 1833
Lord William Bentinck
Most significant charter act. EIC became a purely administrative body — all commercial functions ended. The title changed from "Governor-General of Bengal" to "Governor-General of India" — the first holder being Lord William Bentinck. A Law Member added to the GG's council (first was Macaulay). All laws for British India now required GG Council's approval.

Also: Prohibition of discrimination based on race, religion, colour in public employment (first such provision). A Law Commission was created to codify Indian law.

Constitutional Landmark
1835
English Education Act / Macaulay's Minute
Lord William Bentinck
Macaulay's Minute on Education (1835) argued for English over Sanskrit/Arabic. The Act directed the ₹1 lakh education fund toward English-medium Western education. Created a class of Indians "Indian in blood and colour but English in taste, opinions, morals and intellect."

Long-term effect: Shaped India's educated class for 200 years; produced the nationalist leadership that eventually drove the British out. Deeply controversial — celebrated as modernisation, condemned as cultural erasure.
Education Policy Language Policy
1853
Charter Act of 1853
Lord Dalhousie
Last charter renewal before the 1857 Revolt ended the Company. Charter renewal was for an indefinite period (not 20 years). Introduced competitive examinations for civil services (Indians now legally eligible). Separated legislative and executive functions of the GG's Council for the first time — the legislative council had additional members from provinces.

Notable: First step toward a separate legislative body. Macaulay's influence visible in the civil service reform. This act effectively drafted the blueprint that the Crown would execute in 1858.

Crown Era 1858–1947 Direct rule — from Crown takeover to Independence
The 1857 Pivot: The Revolt of 1857 (Sepoy Mutiny / First War of Independence) was the decisive rupture. The Government of India Act 1858 dissolved the East India Company, transferred its powers to the Crown, and created the post of Secretary of State for India (Cabinet minister) supported by a 15-member Council of India in London, and the Viceroy of India on the ground. The Queen's Proclamation (November 1858) promised non-interference in religion, equality, and good governance — promises honoured variably.
1858
Government of India Act, 1858
Lord Canning · First Viceroy
Abolished the East India Company. All territories, powers and revenues transferred to the Crown. The GG was redesignated Viceroy of India. A Secretary of State for India (Cabinet minister) with a 15-member Council of India took over in London. The Board of Control and Court of Directors were abolished.

Queen's Proclamation: Promised equal treatment of Indians, respect for existing treaties, no further annexations, religious tolerance. In practice, power was further centralised.

1861
Indian Councils Act, 1861
Lord Canning
First Indians admitted to the Viceroy's expanded Legislative Council (nominated). The GG could issue ordinances with immediate effect (6 months). The portfolio system introduced — each council member given charge of a department. Bombay and Madras presidencies restored their legislative councils. Decentralised legislation to provinces.

Significance: First step toward Indian representation in legislation — though purely nominated and advisory.
Indian Reps Portfolio System Decentralisation
1892
Indian Councils Act, 1892
Lord Lansdowne
Enlarged the legislative councils (Central and Provincial). Introduced indirect elections for the first time — though the term "election" was not used. Members could discuss budget and ask questions (but not vote on them). District boards, municipalities, universities, traders' associations could recommend members.

Criticism: Elected members were still a minority; nomination dominated. However, this planted the seed of electoral politics in India.
Indirect Elections Legislative Discussion
1909
Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley-Minto Reforms)
Lord Minto II · Secy of State: John Morley
Significantly enlarged legislative councils. Introduced direct elections for the first time. An Indian (Satyendra Prasanna Sinha) appointed to the Viceroy's Executive Council for the first time. Most controversially, introduced separate electorates for Muslims — a fateful decision that Jinnah and later historians blamed for seeding the Partition.

Context: The Swadeshi movement and partition of Bengal (1905) had inflamed nationalist politics. Morley-Minto was a calculated attempt to split the moderate from the radical Congress while appeasing Muslim League demands.

1919
GOI Act, 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms)
Lord Chelmsford · Secy: Edwin Montagu
Introduced Dyarchy in provinces: subjects split into "Reserved" (law, finance, police — under governor) and "Transferred" (education, agriculture, health — under elected ministers). Created a bicameral central legislature (Council of State + Legislative Assembly). Expanded franchise. Communal representation extended.

Also created: Public Service Commission; Auditor-General; first steps toward responsible government but the most vital powers remained reserved.
Dyarchy Bicameral Legislature Provincial Autonomy
Major Reform
1935
Government of India Act, 1935
Lord Willingdon / Lord Linlithgow
The longest Act in British parliamentary history (321 sections, 10 schedules). Proposed an All-India Federation (never implemented). Granted full provincial autonomy — dyarchy abolished at provincial level, introduced at Centre. Extended franchise to ~35 million. Established Federal Court of India, RBI, Federal Railway Authority. Abolished Council of India (London).

Legacy: Became the working constitution of independent India (1947–1950) with modifications. The 1950 Constitution borrowed extensively from this Act.
Provincial Autonomy Federal Court India's Working Constitution
Most Important Act
1947
Indian Independence Act, 1947
Lord Mountbatten · Last Viceroy
Passed by UK Parliament on 18 July 1947. Partitioned British India into two independent dominions: India and Pakistan (effective 15 August 1947). Dissolved the post of Viceroy. Crown suzerainty over princely states lapsed. Each dominion was free to frame its own constitution. The GOI Act 1935 (as adapted) continued as the interim constitution.

Impact: Ended 190 years of British colonial rule. Accompanied by massive population displacement (15 million), violence, and communal riots claiming 200,000–2 million lives.

Reform Acts Social & Constitutional Social legislation and the slow march toward self-rule
The Reform Paradox: British reform legislation in India served a dual purpose — genuinely improving conditions for Indians while simultaneously legitimising and extending British rule. Social reform (sati abolition, age of consent, caste discrimination) often came from liberal Governors-General working with Indian reformers. Constitutional reforms were harder-fought and always stopped short of full self-governance until 1947.
Social Reform Acts
1829
Bengal Sati Regulation (Reg. XVII, 1829)
Lord William Bentinck
Declared sati (widow immolation) illegal and punishable as culpable homicide. Extended to Bombay (1830) and Madras (1830). Championed by Raja Ram Mohan Roy who had witnessed his sister-in-law's immolation. Fierce orthodox opposition led by Radhakant Deb.

1856
Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856
Lord Dalhousie
Legalised the remarriage of Hindu widows — previously prohibited by custom and considered socially taboo. Advocated by Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar. Provided that widow remarriage was valid and children legitimate. Widow forfeited inheritance rights from former husband upon remarriage.
Widow RightsSocial Reform
1872
Special Marriage Act, 1872
Lord Mayo / Lord Northbrook
Allowed civil marriages outside religious rites — particularly for inter-caste and inter-religion couples. Advocated by Brahmo Samaj. Required both parties to renounce their religion (this controversial clause removed in 1923 revision). First attempt at a secular personal law.
Civil MarriageSecular Law
1891
Age of Consent Act, 1891
Lord Lansdowne
Raised the age of consent for sexual intercourse from 10 to 12 years (for both married and unmarried girls). Triggered after Phulmani Dasi case (1889) — a 10-year-old died from injuries. Bal Gangadhar Tilak opposed it as interference in Hindu tradition. Social reformers like Pandita Ramabai supported it.
Child ProtectionGender Justice
1929
Child Marriage Restraint Act, 1929 (Sarda Act)
Lord Irwin
Fixed minimum age of marriage at 18 for boys and 14 for girls (later raised). Named after Har Bilas Sarda, its proposer. Opposed by traditionalists. First national legislation against child marriage. Gave magistrates powers to prevent impending child marriages.
Child MarriageSarda Act
1937
Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937
Lord Linlithgow
Applied the Muslim personal law (Shariat) to all Muslims in India in matters of marriage, succession, inheritance, and charities. Previously, many Muslim communities (especially in Punjab and NWFP) followed Hindu customary law. Demanded by the Muslim League as recognition of Islamic personal law.

Education & Economic Reform Acts
1854
Wood's Education Despatch, 1854
Lord Dalhousie
"Magna Carta of Indian Education." Sir Charles Wood's despatch established a graded education system: primary schools (vernacular) → high schools (Anglo-vernacular) → colleges (English) → universities. Recommended creation of universities in Calcutta, Bombay, Madras. Department of Education in each province. Grants-in-aid system introduced. Secular focus — universities only teaching and examining, not residential.
University SystemEducation Policy
1857
Indian Universities Act, 1857
Lord Canning
Established the Universities of Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras — the three oldest universities in Asia (founded 24 Jan, 18 Jul, 5 Sep 1857 respectively). Modelled on University of London — affiliating, examining bodies, not teaching. Also established Allahabad University (1887). Transformed Indian higher education.
Universities FoundedHigher Education
1861
Income Tax Act, 1861
Lord Canning
Introduced income tax in India for the first time to recover costs of the 1857 Revolt. Tax on persons earning over ₹200 per annum. A license tax on trades and professions. James Wilson (finance member) designed the system. Wilson died in Calcutta in 1860, having also established the paper currency system and budget system for India.
Income TaxFiscal Reform
1934
Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934
Lord Willingdon
Established the Reserve Bank of India (commenced operations 1 April 1935). Based on Hilton Young Commission recommendations. Brought monetary management under a central bank. Initially privately owned; nationalised in 1949. RBI became the banker to government and banker to banks — the foundation of India's modern monetary system.

Press & Social Freedom vs Control The battle over Indian voices and social fabric
Press as Battleground: The Indian press was the primary vehicle for nationalist thought. British responses oscillated — liberal governors repealed press restrictions; conservative ones reimposed them. The arc from Metcalfe's liberalisation (1835) through the Vernacular Press Act (1878) through Tilak's sedition trials defines this tension. Every major press act was directly connected to a political crisis.
1799
Press Regulations, 1799
Lord Wellesley
First serious censorship regulations. All newspapers required to submit copy to the government secretary before publication. Required newspapers to carry names of editors and printers. War with France cited as justification. Effectively gave the government veto over all news. Remained in force for 30+ years.
Pre-CensorshipPress Control
1823
Press Ordinance, 1823
Lord John Adam (Acting GG)
Required licences for all newspapers. Ram Mohan Roy's Mirat-ul-Akhbar (Persian) and Brahmin Seva Mirat suspended under this. Roy protested and shut down his own paper in protest. This act directly galvanised the Indian free-press movement and became a touchstone for press freedom advocates for generations.
LicensingCensorship
1835
Press Act / Metcalfe's Act, 1835
Lord William Bentinck / Sir Charles Metcalfe (Acting)
Liberated the Indian press. Sir Charles Metcalfe repealed the 1823 licensing requirements. Required only that the printer/publisher file a declaration with the magistrate. No prior censorship. Earned Metcalfe the title "Liberator of the Indian Press." Led to explosion of newspapers in vernacular languages. Bengal, Bombay, and Madras press boomed.
Press FreedomLiberalisation
Press Landmark
1867
Registration of Books Act, 1867
Lord Lawrence
Required registration of every book/newspaper published in British India. Three copies to be deposited with specified authority. Primarily administrative — created a bibliographic record. Replaced earlier registration systems. The foundation of India's copyright and publications registry which persists (much amended) today.
Book RegistrationAdministrative
1878
Vernacular Press Act, 1878
Lord Lytton
The notorious "Gagging Act." Applied specifically to newspapers in Indian languages (not English — a deliberate racial distinction). Magistrates could demand security bonds from editors; could seize printing presses if "seditious" content printed. Passed during the 2nd Afghan War when Lytton wanted no critical coverage.

Response: Amrita Bazar Patrika converted to English overnight to escape the Act. Repealed in 1882 by Lord Ripon.

1908
Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act, 1908
Lord Minto II
Passed after Bal Gangadhar Tilak's sedition trial (Kesari articles). Allowed magistrates to seize printing presses used to publish material inciting violence or murder. Tilak sentenced to 6 years transportation to Burma's Mandalay jail. Widely condemned as targeting nationalist press. Denounced by Gokhale in the Imperial Legislative Council.

1910
Indian Press Act, 1910
Lord Minto II
Required publishers to deposit security (₹500–₹2,000) which could be forfeited if seditious material published. Gave provincial governments power to ban import of foreign seditious publications. Specifically targeted the growing revolutionary press inspired by the Swadeshi movement. Repealed and replaced in 1922, then reimposed in harsher form during WWII.

Repressive
IPC Section 124A — Sedition Law
Introduced by Lord Mayo (1870), Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code criminalised "exciting disaffection towards the Government." Used to prosecute Bal Gangadhar Tilak (1897, 1908), Mahatma Gandhi (1922 — he called it the "prince among the political sections"), Annie Besant, Maulana Azad, and scores of others. Gandhi accepted the charge proudly: "I am here therefore to invite and cheerfully submit to the highest penalty that can be inflicted upon me." The law survived into independent India (Article 19 challenge) and remains controversial to date.
Judicial Courts & Codes From the Supreme Court to the Federal Court — building colonial justice
The Colonial Legal System: British India built a parallel legal system on top of existing Mughal and regional structures. English Common Law mixed with codified personal laws for Hindus and Muslims. The IPC (1860) and CPC are Macaulay's lasting contributions — still largely in force. High Courts replaced the old Supreme Courts and Sadar Diwani Adalats in 1861. The Federal Court (1937) became the Supreme Court of India in 1950.
1773
Supreme Court of Calcutta (Regulating Act)
Warren Hastings
Established the Supreme Court of Judicature at Fort William, Calcutta — the first British court in India. Chief Justice: Sir Elijah Impey. Had jurisdiction over all British subjects in Bengal. Famously came into conflict with the GG's Council over its jurisdiction — the Patna Case (1778) and the Raja Nand Kumar affair (Impey hanged Nand Kumar, accused of Warren Hastings' enemy, in 1775 — deeply controversial).
First Supreme Court1773
1860
Indian Penal Code, 1860
Lord Canning
Drafted by Macaulay's First Law Commission (1834–37), finalised by Barnes Peacock. Came into force 1 January 1862. Comprehensive criminal code covering offences against the state, body, property, marriage, religion, elections. Still substantially in force (now renamed BNS 2023 in India). 511 sections, clear definitions, graded punishments. Replaced fragmented Presidency criminal laws.

1861
High Courts Act, 1861
Lord Canning
Replaced the Supreme Courts (at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras) and the Sadar Adalats with High Courts. High Courts of Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras established. Combined the jurisdiction of the old courts. Power to make rules of procedure. Original, appellate, and criminal jurisdiction. Allahabad HC followed in 1866, others later. Created the structure of India's current High Court system.
High CourtsCourt Reform
1872
Indian Evidence Act, 1872
Lord Mayo / Lord Northbrook
Drafted by Sir James Fitzjames Stephen. Codified the rules of evidence in all Indian courts. 167 sections covering relevancy, admissibility, oral and documentary evidence, witnesses, estoppel, burden of proof. Replaced highly inconsistent evidence rules across presidencies. Still largely in force today (renamed Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023).
Evidence LawStill in Force
1882
Transfer of Property Act, 1882
Lord Ripon
Codified laws governing property transfers — sales, mortgages, leases, exchanges, gifts, actionable claims. Replaced inconsistent English common law and local custom. Introduced the concept of "notice" and "bona fide purchaser." Still substantially in force in India. One of the cornerstones of Indian property law — every real estate transaction today is governed by its principles.
Property LawStill in Force
1935
Federal Court of India (GOI Act 1935)
Lord Willingdon
Established the Federal Court of India (became operational 1937) as the apex judicial body. Original jurisdiction in federal disputes; appellate jurisdiction from High Courts in constitutional matters. Appeal lay to the Privy Council in London. Chief Justice: Sir Maurice Gwyer (first). Became the Supreme Court of India on 28 January 1950.

Military & Security Control & Coercion Disarming, detaining, and dominating — the security state
The Security Architecture: Maintaining control over 300 million people required a legal infrastructure of coercion. The Arms Act, Sedition Laws, and Preventive Detention laws formed the backbone. Most controversially, the Rowlatt Act (1919) — allowing detention without trial — triggered Gandhi's first nationwide satyagraha and directly led to the Jallianwala Bagh massacre. Many of these laws survived independence and were used by post-independence governments against their own citizens.
1857
Indian (New) Penal Code — Mutiny Background
Lord Canning
The IPC was drafted before 1857 but its provisions on waging war against the government (Section 121), sedition (124A), and unlawful assembly were directly shaped by the 1857 experience. The Mutiny Act allowed courts martial for civilians in disturbed areas. After 1857, a comprehensive counter-insurgency legal framework was built into the IPC itself.
Waging WarPost-Mutiny
1878
Arms Act, 1878
Lord Lytton
Prohibited Indians from possessing arms without a licence issued by a British official. Europeans were exempt. Directly disarmed the Indian population after 1857. Gandhi called it one of the most humiliating laws — "it is we taking away their manliness." Amended and replaced by Arms Act 1959 after independence. Bhagat Singh's acquisition of arms was technically a violation of this Act.

1908
Explosive Substances Act, 1908
Lord Minto II
Passed after the Muzaffarpur bomb attack (1908) by Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki (targeted magistrate Kingsford but killed two British women). Made possession and manufacture of explosives a serious criminal offence with harsh sentences. Used extensively to prosecute revolutionary nationalists — Bhagat Singh charged under this Act for the Delhi Assembly bombing (1929).
Anti-TerrorismRevolutionary Control
1919
Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919 (Rowlatt Act)
Lord Chelmsford
Based on the Sedition Committee Report (Rowlatt Committee). Allowed detention without trial for up to 2 years for political prisoners. No jury trial; suspects could be confined without charge. Indians called it the "Black Act" and "No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal."

Gandhi launched his first nationwide satyagraha (6 April 1919 hartal). Led directly to Jallianwala Bagh massacre (13 April 1919 — 379+ killed per official figures, 1,000+ per Indian estimates). Repealed in 1922 but its legacy defined Indian nationalism's turn to mass mobilisation.
Detention Without TrialBlack Act
Most Hated Law
1939
Defence of India Act, 1939
Lord Linlithgow
Wartime emergency legislation passed at the outbreak of WWII. Gave the government sweeping powers to detain, censor, and commandeer resources. Used to arrest virtually the entire Congress leadership (Nehru, Patel, Azad) after the Quit India Movement (1942). Over 60,000 Congress members arrested under Defence of India Rules during 1942-44. Also regulated prices and controlled economy.

Wartime Repression
Jallianwala Bagh — The Consequence of the Rowlatt Act
On 13 April 1919, Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed crowd gathered at Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar for the Baisakhi festival, unaware of a prohibition order under the Rowlatt crackdown. Minimum 379 dead (official), over 1,200 by Indian National Congress estimates. The crowd had no exit — troops fired 1,650 rounds. The event permanently alienated moderate Indian opinion from British rule. Rabindranath Tagore returned his knighthood. Winston Churchill called it "monstrous." General Dyer was forced to resign but received a £26,000 "sword of honour" subscription from British India sympathisers.

Repressive vs Reform: The Pendulum
Pattern: British India's legislative history shows a clear pendulum between liberal and repressive phases, often tied to which party governed in Britain. Whig/Liberal governors (Bentinck, Ripon, Minto with Morley) pushed reform. Conservative governors (Lytton, Dyer era, post-WWI) pushed coercion. Indian nationalism accelerated reforms but each reform came too late and fell too short — widening the credibility gap until independence became inevitable.
Liberal Phase
Bentinck era (1828–35): Sati abolished, press freed, English education, widow remarriage debate.
Ripon era (1880–84): Vernacular Press Act repealed, Local Self-Government, Ilbert Bill (controversial).
Morley-Minto (1905–10): First Indian in executive council, direct elections.
Repressive Phase
Wellesley era (1798–1805): Press censorship, expansionism.
Lytton era (1876–80): Vernacular Press Act, Arms Act, Grand Durbar during famine.
Chelmsford era (1916–21): Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh — the most damaging phase for British credibility.
Transitional Phase
Dalhousie era (1848–56): Progressive (railways, telegraph, education) but aggressive (Doctrine of Lapse).
Post-1935 era: Provincial autonomy worked (1937 elections) but WWII and partition overshadowed constitutional progress.
